\chapter{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{chap:conclusions}

This dissertation set out to close a specific gap in the RA-TDMAs+ framework.
The framework already provided collision-free, slot-based medium access and
disseminated a connectivity matrix among the nodes, but it had no routing layer
to turn that topology knowledge into multi-hop forwarding for arbitrary IP
traffic (Section~\ref{sec:problem}). The objective set out in
Chapter~\ref{chap:intro} was to supply that missing layer, a routing mechanism
that enables peer-to-peer IP communication between any two nodes regardless of
the current topology, while leaving intact the TDMA slot discipline on which the
whole framework rests. Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation} described the design and
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} validated it on real hardware. This section draws
the overall conclusions. The directions the work opens up are developed
afterwards under Future Work.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

The specific objectives defined in Chapter~\ref{chap:intro} were met. A
link-quality metric derived from TDMA beacon-reception statistics was defined and
used both to weight the spanning tree and to trigger relay activation. A
topology-aware routing algorithm derives next-hop decisions from a Minimum
Spanning Tree over that graph and installs the resulting routes into the Linux
kernel over Netlink. The relay forwarding was integrated with the IP-forwarding
engine of the kernel through a dual routing-table architecture, so that once a
transit packet is re-injected into the TUN interface the kernel itself forwards
it between interfaces. And the
communication stack was split into a UDP control plane for the topology beacons
and a TCP data plane for application data, giving reliable in-order delivery of
video without altering the beacon mechanism inherited from the base framework.
Practical experimentation then confirmed that these parts work together end to
end.

The significance of the result is as much architectural as quantitative. By
routing at Layer~3 with kernel forwarding over a TCP data plane, the system
reaches the same TDMA-level behaviour as the earlier Layer~2 approach of
Morais~\cite{morais2024synchronization} while operating entirely through standard
operating-system interfaces. The forwarding path is visible to ordinary IP tools,
the data plane delivers video reliably and in order, and the relay is, in effect,
transparent to the application. It therefore stands as a distinct and more
observable alternative to the Layer~2 predecessor, solving the same multi-hop
problem by different means and showing that a kernel-integrated,
connection-oriented data plane can coexist with strict TDMA slotting. For the
scenario that motivated the work, a mobile robot streaming live video to a remote
operator over a network the nodes build themselves, this is the outcome that
matters. When a direct link is lost, connectivity that a single-hop system could
not have kept is restored automatically and the stream continues.

Not every goal was carried through to completion. The core objectives were met,
but a few threads were left open for want of time. The clearest is the comparison
with the Layer~2 method, which could not be fully closed: the re-implementation
was reconstructed from the published description rather than the original source,
so the streaming instability it showed cannot yet be cleanly separated into what
is intrinsic to the design, what is an artefact of the shared-domain testbed, and
what is a limitation of the reconstruction. A few refinements were likewise left
unmeasured, such as running the system with a shorter \texttt{MAX\_AGE}. More
broadly, the evaluation made clear where the design can be taken further: the
objectives were met within deliberate engineering trade-offs, and exercising the
system, on the bench and then in motion, brought out which of those trade-offs
are most worth revisiting. The remainder of this chapter develops these
directions.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

\subsection{Wire-Level Slot Containment}
\label{subsec:fw-slot-containment}

The clearest limitation surfaced by the evaluation concerns \emph{when} data
leaves the antenna. The framework controls the \emph{volume} offered per slot,
through the per-slot byte budget and back-pressure of
Section~\ref{sec:slot-control}, but not the exact transmit instant, because below
the TCP socket the kernel schedules transmission on its own terms. A batch
offered near the slot end can therefore reach the air slightly into the next
slot, the bounded incursion of Section~\ref{subsubsec:video-slot}, which is the
\emph{packet spreading} that Pinto and Almeida evidenced as a protocol-level
problem~\cite{pinto2018robust, ramos2018aerial}.

Several refinements would tighten the boundary, from least to most invasive. The
byte budget can be set conservatively below the slot capacity and calibrated
against on-air measurement. The packets can be paced across the slot instead of
being burst at its start. The unsent data the kernel may hold can be bounded so
that it does not transmit a late tail, the socket-queue cap Pinto used to reduce
packet spreading and which our TCP data plane does not yet impose
(\texttt{TCP\_NOTSENT\_LOWAT}, a small \texttt{SO\_SNDBUF}). Most thoroughly, the
slot gate can be moved below TCP, emitting the slot-critical traffic as raw
frames timed to the slot, which recovers the wire-level timing the connectionless
Layer~2 design has by construction at the cost of re-introducing the complexity
the TCP plane was chosen to avoid. Quantifying how much each refinement reduces
the incursions, with on-air measurement, is a direct continuation of this work.

A complementary approach relaxes the equal-slot assumption itself. The incursions
arise partly because N1, the only heavy source, must fit its whole burst into the
same fixed slot granted to the near-idle N2 and N3. A variable slot-length
scheme, in the spirit of the Distributed Variable Slot-length Protocol (DVSP) of
Pinto~\cite{ramos2018aerial}, keeps the round period constant but sizes each slot
to the load of its node, giving the source a longer slot and the relay and sink
shorter ones. This attacks the cause rather than the symptom, although it still
cannot fix the exact on-air instant, which remains with the kernel.

\subsection{Faster Convergence}
\label{subsec:fw-convergence}

Recovery after a link failure is dominated by the node-aging timeout
(\texttt{MAX\_AGE} $= 2.0$\,s) used to detect a dead direct link
(Section~\ref{subsec:res-convergence}), the routing computation itself being
negligible. Reducing \texttt{MAX\_AGE}, or replacing the fixed timeout with an
adaptive scheme that reacts to the recent beacon-loss rate, would speed up
recovery at the cost of robustness to transient beacon loss in the shared
collision domain.

A more fundamental gap concerns a link that degrades unevenly across the two
planes. The lightweight UDP beacons can keep arriving after the heavier TCP data
plane has already broken (Section~\ref{subsec:res-demo}), so the topology still
believes the direct link is healthy while the operator has already lost the video
and the command channel, and the system does not reroute. Closing this gap needs
a \emph{data-plane liveness signal}, treating a stalled or failed TCP connection
to the next hop, like a missed beacon, as evidence to suppress the link and force
the MST to reroute. This is implementable within the current architecture, since
the data plane already observes its own send failures, and it directly targets
the dominant cost analysed in Section~\ref{subsec:res-convergence}, the
source-side wind-down in which the transmitter can stall on the dead socket up to
the one-second send timeout before the stream settles onto the relay.

\subsection{Physical-Layer Link Quality and RSSI}
\label{subsec:fw-rssi}

The routing metric used here is the per-link packet delivery ratio (PDR) derived
from beacon statistics (Section~\ref{subsec:res-slots}), which drives both the MST
edge weights and relay activation. PDR is robust but lagging, registering a
failing link only after enough beacons are lost, the same delay that dominates
convergence. A physical-layer signal such as the received signal strength
indicator (RSSI) would complement it by reacting before packets are lost, letting
the MST shift weight toward a stronger path pre-emptively rather than detecting
the break after the fact. Incorporating RSSI was attempted but proved impractical,
because the Raspberry~Pi adapters do not expose a reliable per-neighbour RSSI in
ad-hoc (IBSS) mode, and RSSI is in any case noisy and would have to be filtered
and combined with PDR rather than trusted alone. With suitable hardware, an
RSSI-and-PDR composite weight on the MST, together with the data-plane liveness
signal above, would give a faster and more certain reaction to the degradations
that matter most for a mobile teleoperation link.

\subsection{Multi-Hop Scalability}
\label{subsec:fw-multihop}

The evaluation used a three-node testbed, the minimum that exercises a relay. The
forwarding step is identical at every relay, a kernel IP-forward of the
unmodified datagram (Section~\ref{sec:pkt-flow}), so the design generalises to
longer paths without change. What grows with the hop count is the per-hop slot
latency and the capacity sharing inherent to a single-channel multi-hop chain. A
systematic study on a larger testbed, ideally with nodes that cannot all hear one
another, would characterise these effects and separate them from the artefacts of
a single shared collision domain.

\subsection{Closing the Layer~2 Comparison}
\label{subsec:fw-l2}

The side-by-side re-implementation of the Layer~2 method
(Section~\ref{subsec:l2-rerun}) was reconstructed from the published description
rather than the original source. Separating how much of the streaming instability
it showed is intrinsic to the non-slotted relay, how much is an artefact of the
shared-domain testbed and the software-induced failure, and how much is a
limitation of the reconstruction, would put the comparison on an even firmer
footing.
