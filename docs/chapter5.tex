\chapter{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{chap:conclusions}

This dissertation set out to close a specific gap in the RA-TDMAs+ framework,
which provided collision-free slot-based medium access and a shared connectivity
matrix but no routing layer to turn that topology knowledge into multi-hop
forwarding for arbitrary IP traffic (Section~\ref{sec:problem}). The objective set
out in Chapter~\ref{chap:intro} was to supply that layer, enabling peer-to-peer IP
communication between any two nodes regardless of the current topology while
preserving the TDMA slot discipline on which the framework rests.
Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation} described the design and
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} validated it on real hardware. This section draws
the overall conclusions. The directions the work opens up follow under Future
Work.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

The specific objectives defined in Chapter~\ref{chap:intro} were met. A
link-quality metric derived from beacon-reception statistics weights the spanning
tree and triggers relay activation. A topology-aware algorithm derives next-hop
decisions from a Minimum Spanning Tree over that graph and installs the resulting
routes into the Linux kernel over Netlink. The relay forwarding was integrated
with the kernel IP-forwarding engine through a dual routing-table architecture, so
that once a transit packet is re-injected into the TUN interface the kernel
forwards it between interfaces. And the communication stack was split into a UDP
control plane for the topology beacons and a TCP data plane for application data,
giving reliable in-order delivery of video without altering the beacon mechanism
inherited from the base framework. Practical experimentation confirmed that these
parts work together end to end.

The significance of the result is as much architectural as quantitative. By
routing at Layer~3 with kernel forwarding over a TCP data plane, the system
reaches the same TDMA-level behaviour as the earlier Layer~2 approach of
Morais~\cite{morais2024synchronization} while operating entirely through standard
operating-system interfaces: the forwarding path is visible to ordinary IP tools,
the data plane delivers video reliably and in order, and the relay is transparent
to the application. It therefore stands as a distinct and more observable
alternative to the Layer~2 predecessor, showing that a kernel-integrated,
connection-oriented data plane can coexist with strict TDMA slotting. For the
scenario that motivated the work, a mobile robot streaming live video to a remote
operator over a network the nodes build themselves, this is the outcome that
matters: when a direct link is lost, connectivity that a single-hop system could
not have kept is restored automatically and the stream continues.

Not everything was carried through to completion, mostly for lack of time. The
core objectives were met, but a few threads were left open. The main one is the
slot-incursion problem of Section~\ref{subsubsec:video-slot}: considerable effort
went into containing the video burst within the slot at the wire level, without
success, so it remains the clearest open question
(Section~\ref{subsec:fw-slot-containment}). A shorter \texttt{MAX\_AGE} was also
tried and showed some instability, but the experiment was not instrumented to
quantify it. And the comparison with the Layer~2 method rests on a reconstruction
from the published description and could not be fully closed
(Section~\ref{subsec:fw-l2}). More broadly, exercising the system on the bench and
then in motion brought out which of its deliberate trade-offs are most worth
revisiting. The remainder of this chapter develops these directions.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

\subsection{Wire-Level Slot Containment}
\label{subsec:fw-slot-containment}

The clearest limitation concerns \emph{when} data leaves the antenna. The
framework bounds the \emph{volume} offered per slot, through the per-slot byte
budget and back-pressure of Section~\ref{sec:slot-control}, but not the exact
transmit instant, because below the TCP socket the kernel schedules transmission
on its own terms. A batch offered near the slot end can therefore reach the air
slightly into the next slot, the bounded incursion of
Section~\ref{subsubsec:video-slot}, which is the \emph{packet spreading} that
Pinto and Almeida evidenced as a protocol-level
problem~\cite{pinto2018robust, ramos2018aerial}.

Several refinements would tighten the boundary, from least to most invasive:
calibrating the byte budget conservatively below the slot capacity, pacing the
packets across the slot rather than bursting them at its start, bounding the
unsent data the kernel may hold so it does not emit a late tail
(\texttt{TCP\_NOTSENT\_LOWAT} with a small \texttt{SO\_SNDBUF}, the socket-queue
cap Pinto used and which our data plane does not yet impose), or, most thoroughly,
moving the slot gate below TCP and emitting the slot-critical traffic as raw frames
timed to the slot. That last option recovers the wire-level timing the
connectionless Layer~2 design has by construction, at the cost of re-introducing
the complexity the TCP plane was chosen to avoid. A complementary route relaxes
the equal-slot assumption itself: because N1, the only heavy source, must fit its
whole burst into the same slot granted to the near-idle N2 and N3, a variable
slot-length scheme in the spirit of the Distributed Variable Slot-length Protocol
(DVSP) of Pinto~\cite{ramos2018aerial} would size each slot to the load of its
node, attacking the cause rather than the symptom. None of these fixes the exact
on-air instant, which stays with the kernel. Quantifying how much each reduces the
incursions, with on-air measurement, is a direct continuation of this work.

\subsection{Faster Convergence}
\label{subsec:fw-convergence}

Recovery after a link failure is dominated by the node-aging timeout
(\texttt{MAX\_AGE} $= 2.0$\,s) that detects a dead direct link
(Section~\ref{subsec:res-convergence}), the routing computation being negligible.
Reducing \texttt{MAX\_AGE}, or replacing the fixed timeout with an adaptive scheme
that reacts to the recent beacon-loss rate, would speed up recovery at the cost of
robustness to transient beacon loss in the shared collision domain.

A more fundamental gap concerns a link that degrades unevenly across the two
planes: the lightweight UDP beacons can keep arriving after the heavier TCP data
plane has broken (Section~\ref{subsec:res-demo}), so the topology still believes
the direct link healthy while the operator has already lost the video and the
command channel, and the system does not reroute. Closing this gap needs a
\emph{data-plane liveness signal} that treats a stalled or failed TCP connection
to the next hop, like a missed beacon, as evidence to suppress the link and force
the MST to reroute. It is implementable within the current architecture, since the
data plane already observes its own send failures, and it targets the dominant
cost analysed in Section~\ref{subsec:res-convergence}, the source-side wind-down
in which the transmitter can stall on the dead socket up to the one-second send
timeout before the stream settles onto the relay.

\subsection{Physical-Layer Link Quality and RSSI}
\label{subsec:fw-rssi}

The routing metric used here is the per-link packet delivery ratio (PDR) from
beacon statistics (Section~\ref{subsec:res-slots}), which drives both the MST edge
weights and relay activation. PDR is robust but lagging, registering a failing
link only after enough beacons are lost, the same delay that dominates
convergence. A physical-layer signal such as the received signal strength
indicator (RSSI) would react before packets are lost, letting the MST shift weight
toward a stronger path pre-emptively. Incorporating RSSI was attempted but proved
impractical, because the Raspberry~Pi adapters do not expose a reliable
per-neighbour RSSI in ad-hoc (IBSS) mode, and RSSI is in any case noisy and would
have to be filtered and combined with PDR rather than trusted alone. With suitable
hardware, an RSSI-and-PDR composite weight on the MST, together with the
data-plane liveness signal above, would give a faster and more certain reaction to
the degradations that matter most for a mobile teleoperation link.

\subsection{Multi-Hop Scalability}
\label{subsec:fw-multihop}

The evaluation used a three-node testbed, the minimum that exercises a relay. The
forwarding step is identical at every relay, a kernel IP-forward of the unmodified
datagram (Section~\ref{sec:pkt-flow}), so the design generalises to longer paths
without change. What grows with the hop count is the per-hop slot latency and the
capacity sharing inherent to a single-channel multi-hop chain. A systematic study
on a larger testbed, ideally with nodes that cannot all hear one another, would
characterise these effects and separate them from the artefacts of a single
shared collision domain.

\subsection{Closing the Layer~2 Comparison}
\label{subsec:fw-l2}

The side-by-side re-implementation of the Layer~2 method
(Section~\ref{subsec:l2-rerun}) was reconstructed from the published description
rather than the original source. Separating how much of the streaming instability
it showed is intrinsic to the non-slotted relay, how much is an artefact of the
shared-domain testbed and the software-induced failure, and how much is a
limitation of the reconstruction, would put the comparison on an even firmer
footing.
