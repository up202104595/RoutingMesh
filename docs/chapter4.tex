\chapter{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{chap:evaluation}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:eval-intro}

This chapter presents the experimental evaluation of the Layer~3 routing
system described in Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}. The evaluation was
carried out on physical hardware in a three-node testbed that reproduces the
target operational scenario: a mobile robot transmitting a live video stream
to a stationary base station, with an intermediate relay node available to
maintain connectivity after a direct-link failure.

The evaluation pursues two objectives. The first is to characterise the
end-to-end performance of the system: control-channel latency, video
throughput, and recovery time after a link failure. The second, and central
to this dissertation, is to \emph{prove that the nodes respect their TDMA
slots} when transporting both periodic synchronisation beacons and a
continuous video stream, and that this property is preserved when traffic is
forwarded through the relay.

The chapter is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:setup} describes the
hardware, the network and software configuration, the video pipeline, and the
link-failure methodology. Section~\ref{sec:metrics} defines the performance
metrics. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents and analyses each result in a
dedicated subsection. Section~\ref{sec:comparison} compares the system with
the Layer~2 approach of Morais~\cite{morais2024synchronization}.
Section~\ref{sec:eval-summary} summarises the findings.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Testbed Overview}
\label{subsec:overview}

The testbed comprises three nodes, each with a fixed role. The network is an
IEEE~802.11 ad-hoc (IBSS) cell on channel~6. Two addressing planes coexist:
a \emph{physical} plane (\texttt{172.20.10.0/28}) carried by the wireless
interface, and a \emph{mesh} plane (\texttt{10.0.0.0/24}) carried by a TUN
virtual interface, through which all application traffic is routed by the
TDMA framework. The TDMA schedule uses a frame of $T_{\mathrm{frame}} =
150$\,ms divided into three slots of $T_{\mathrm{slot}} = 50$\,ms, one per
node, with a 5\,ms guard interval at the end of each slot. Slot~$i$ is
assigned to node~$i$, giving the fixed ordering
$[\text{N1}, \text{N2}, \text{N3}]$ shown in Figure~\ref{fig:frame}.

\vspace{0.4cm}
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\fbox{\parbox{0.95\textwidth}{\centering\vspace{1.4cm}
N1 slot $[0,50)$\,ms \qquad N2 slot $[50,100)$\,ms \qquad N3 slot $[100,150)$\,ms
\vspace{1.4cm}}}
\caption{TDMA frame structure with three synchronised nodes. The 150\,ms frame
is divided into three 50\,ms slots; each node transmits only within its own
slot.}
\label{fig:frame}
\end{figure}
\vspace{0.5cm}

All three nodes were placed on a laboratory bench within mutual radio range,
so that any pair could communicate directly. This is a deliberate constraint:
because the nodes share a single collision domain, link failures cannot be
produced by distance and must instead be induced in software
(Section~\ref{subsec:linkfail}). The same limitation was reported by
Morais~\cite{morais2024synchronization}.

Two distinct traffic planes travel over the air, and they must not be confused
when interpreting the captures:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Synchronisation beacons (control).} Each node broadcasts one
  beacon per frame, \emph{within its own slot}, to its neighbours as a small
  UDP packet (physical addresses, ports 7001--7003, $\approx 93$--$107$\,B).
  These carry the adjacency matrix and slot-timing information used by the
  RA-TDMAs+ synchroniser. They are \emph{not} relayed: every node emits its
  own.
  \item \textbf{Application data.} The video stream and the control channel
  travel over the mesh plane (\texttt{10.0.0.x}). The video is an MPEG-TS/UDP
  flow to \texttt{10.0.0.3:5000}; the teleoperation RTT probe is a UDP echo to
  \texttt{10.0.0.1:19876}. When the direct link is unavailable, this traffic
  is forwarded through N2.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Link-Failure Methodology}
\label{subsec:linkfail}

Because all nodes share a collision domain, a direct-link failure is induced
logically rather than physically. At the moment of failure, the following
rules are applied on N3:

\begin{verbatim}
iptables -I INPUT  1 -s 172.20.10.1 -j DROP
iptables -I OUTPUT 1 -d 172.20.10.1 -j DROP
\end{verbatim}

These rules discard, at the IP layer, every frame to and from N1's physical
address, making the direct link invisible to N3's TDMA framework and to the
TCP data plane, and forcing traffic onto the relay path
N1\,$\to$\,N2\,$\to$\,N3. The rules are cleared between repetitions with
\texttt{iptables -F}.

A subtlety relevant to the synchronisation analysis
(Section~\ref{subsec:res-slots}) is that \texttt{iptables} filters at the IP
layer, whereas a packet \emph{capture} (\texttt{tcpdump}) taps the interface
\emph{before} the firewall. A capture taken at N3 with the link blocked
therefore still records N1's beacons (they reach the antenna), even though
N3's TDMA application no longer receives them. The block changes which node N3
synchronises to (it falls back to synchronising through N2), without removing
N1's transmissions from the medium. To confirm that the block was effective
for the data plane, each relay measurement was preceded by a check that N1's
physical address was unreachable (\texttt{ping} to \texttt{172.20.10.1}
failing), since the physical address --- unlike the mesh address --- is not
reachable through the relay.

\section{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

\paragraph{Round-trip time (RTT).}
$\mathrm{RTT}_k = t^{\mathrm{rx}}_k - t^{\mathrm{tx}}_k$, the elapsed time from
N3 sending a UDP echo request to receiving N1's reply. In a TDMA network the
RTT reflects the slot structure: each leg of the round trip can depart only
within its node's slot.

\paragraph{Packet delivery ratio (PDR).}
$\mathrm{PDR} = N_{\mathrm{rx}}/N_{\mathrm{tx}} \times 100\,\%$, the fraction
of echo requests answered within a timeout.

\paragraph{Jitter.}
$J = \frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{k=2}^{N}\lvert \mathrm{RTT}_k - \mathrm{RTT}_{k-1}\rvert$,
the mean absolute deviation of consecutive RTT samples.

\paragraph{Throughput.}
$T = 8\,B_{\mathrm{total}} / \Delta t$ [kbps], where $B_{\mathrm{total}}$ is
the sum of UDP video payload bytes received in an observation window
$\Delta t$.

\paragraph{Convergence time.}
$t_{\mathrm{conv}} = t_{\mathrm{first\,relay}} - t_{\mathrm{last\,direct}}$,
the gap between the last video packet on the direct path and the first packet
recovered through the relay.

\paragraph{Inter-arrival time (IA).}
$\mathrm{IA}_k = t_{k+1} - t_k$, the time between consecutive video packets.
Its distribution reveals the TDMA burst structure and allows verification that
relay forwarding preserves it.

\paragraph{Slot occupancy.}
The transmission instant of each captured beacon is wrapped to the frame,
$\phi = t \bmod T_{\mathrm{frame}}$, and attributed to its source node. A node
respects its slot if its beacons cluster within its assigned 50\,ms interval.
Because the RA-TDMAs+ synchroniser advances all slots by a common amount each
round, the whole frame rotates slowly in wall-clock time; this common rotation
is removed before evaluating the per-node clustering, leaving the
\emph{relative} synchronisation between nodes.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

This section is organised so that each subsection isolates one property of the
system: it states briefly how the corresponding test was performed, presents
the figure that supports the result, and analyses it immediately afterwards.

\subsection{Control-Channel Latency}
\label{subsec:res-rtt}

To characterise control-plane latency, N3 sent 100 UDP echo requests to
\texttt{10.0.0.1} at 10\,Hz, with N1 replying to each immediately; the 100
round-trip samples were recorded at N3. The test was performed over the direct
link and over the relay path. Figure~\ref{fig:rtt-direct} shows the result for
the direct link.

\vspace{0.4cm}
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{RTT_over_the_direct_link.png}
\caption{Control-channel RTT over the direct link (N3\,$\to$\,N1, 100 pings,
PDR $= 100\,\%$). \textbf{Left:} RTT per ping sequence; the values alternate
in blocks between a low cluster ($\approx 50$\,ms, blue) and a high cluster
($\approx 200$\,ms, red). \textbf{Right:} the resulting distribution is
strongly bimodal, with the two clusters separated by exactly one TDMA frame
(150\,ms) and an empty band in between --- the signature of TDMA round
quantisation.}
\label{fig:rtt-direct}
\end{figure}
\vspace{0.5cm}

The RTT does not concentrate around a single value; it splits into two
well-separated clusters with an empty band between roughly 60 and 190\,ms.
This is a direct consequence of the slot structure. A round trip traverses two
slot waits: the request waits for N3's slot, and the reply waits for N1's
slot. Because each leg can depart only within its node's slot, the reply
either reaches N1's transmit opportunity in the immediately following frame
--- giving the lower cluster ($\approx 52$\,ms, set by the fixed encapsulation
and the once-per-frame transmission overhead) --- or narrowly misses it and
must wait one entire frame, giving the upper cluster ($\approx 52 + 150 =
202$\,ms). The cluster separation of exactly one frame (150\,ms) is the
signature of TDMA round quantisation: latency is lost only in whole-frame
units, which is why no values fall between the clusters. The proportion of
pings in each cluster varies between runs, because the free-running 10\,Hz
probe cadence beats against the 150\,ms frame; the mean and median are
therefore not representative single numbers, and the two clusters are reported
separately instead (Table~\ref{tab:rtt}).

The same test over the relay path is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rtt-relay}.

\vspace{0.4cm}
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{RTT_over_the_relay_path.png}
\caption{Control-channel RTT over the relay path
(N3\,$\to$\,N2\,$\to$\,N1, 100 pings, PDR $= 100\,\%$). The distribution is
practically identical to the direct link in Figure~\ref{fig:rtt-direct}: the
same two clusters, the same one-frame separation. Relaying therefore adds no
significant latency to the control channel.}
\label{fig:rtt-relay}
\end{figure}
\vspace{0.5cm}

The relay distribution is practically identical to the direct one: the same
two clusters and the same one-frame separation. Forwarding the control packet
through N2 adds no significant latency, which is consistent with the
throughput result of the next subsection --- the kernel forwarding engine at
N2 relays each packet within the same slot in which it arrives. The 100\,\%
PDR and the bounded maximum (below two frames) in both conditions confirm that
the slots are respected: a desynchronised network would instead exhibit
unbounded, unstructured latency and packet loss, whereas the clean two-level
quantisation observed here is positive evidence that the schedule holds.
Normalising by the frame period, the mean RTT of $\approx 0.9$ frame is
consistent with the value reported by Morais~\cite{morais2024synchronization}
(0.88 frame), confirming equivalent TDMA-level latency behaviour.

\subsection{Video Throughput}
\label{subsec:res-throughput}

Video throughput was measured by streaming continuously from N1 to N3 and
recording every video packet with a passive capture at N3 for 15\,s; because
the capture does not occupy port~5000, it does not perturb the live stream.
The test was repeated three times over the direct link and three times over
the relay path. Figure~\ref{fig:throughput} and Table~\ref{tab:throughput}
report the means.

The direct-link mean of 558\,kbps slightly exceeds the 500\,kbps encoder
target for two reasons. First, the MPEG-TS container adds $\approx 15\,\%$
overhead (a 4-byte header per 188-byte transport packet). Second, the encoder
uses \texttt{-g~1}, disabling inter-frame prediction; every frame is an
independent intra frame, producing a higher and more constant bitrate than a
configuration with P/B-frames.

Under relay conditions the mean throughput is 552\,kbps, a reduction of only
$\approx 1\,\%$. This is within the run-to-run variability of the encoder as
scene content changes, and no systematic degradation attributable to the relay
hop is observed. The relay is therefore effectively \emph{transparent} for the
video plane: kernel forwarding at N2 carries the stream without consuming a
separate TDMA scheduling step. This is the data-plane counterpart of the RTT
result, where the relay likewise added no measurable latency.

\subsection{Routing Convergence}
\label{subsec:res-convergence}

Convergence is the time the system takes to restore the video stream through
the relay after the direct link fails. It was measured entirely at N3 from the
passive video capture, as follows. First, the direct-link stream was allowed
to stabilise for at least three seconds. The \texttt{iptables} rules of
Section~\ref{subsec:linkfail} were then applied to sever the direct link. The
capture was monitored for two events: the \emph{gap}, defined as the instant
no video packet had arrived for more than $0.4$\,s (its timestamp,
$t_{\mathrm{last\,direct}}$, marks the last packet delivered over the direct
path); and the \emph{recovery}, defined as the arrival of the first video
packet after the gap, forwarded through N2 ($t_{\mathrm{first\,relay}}$). The
convergence time is
\begin{equation}
t_{\mathrm{conv}} = t_{\mathrm{first\,relay}} - t_{\mathrm{last\,direct}}.
\label{eq:tconv}
\end{equation}
The direct link was then restored and the rules cleared before the next
repetition. Three repetitions were performed; Table~\ref{tab:convergence}
reports the results.

\midrule

\vspace{0.5cm}

The median convergence time of the two repetitions that produced a measurable
gap is $\approx 2.8$\,s, equivalent to about 18~TDMA frames. In the third
repetition the relay route was still installed in the routing tables from the
previous repetition, so the stream switched paths in less than the $0.4$\,s
detection threshold and no gap was recorded; this case is reported separately
and excluded from the median.

The dominant component of the convergence time is the \emph{link-failure
detection}, which is governed by the node-aging mechanism of the RA-TDMAs+
adjacency matrix. Each node keeps, for every neighbour, the timestamp of the
last beacon received from it; the neighbour's \emph{age} is the elapsed time
since that beacon. On every round the function \texttt{removeDeadLinks()}
compares each age against the constant \texttt{MAX\_AGE}, and a neighbour whose
age reaches \texttt{MAX\_AGE} is declared dead and removed from the matrix. In
this implementation \texttt{MAX\_AGE} $= 2.0$\,s, so when the direct link to N1
is severed, N3 stops receiving N1's beacons and continues to treat N1 as a live
neighbour for a full $2.0$\,s before the link is removed. Only then does the
topology change and the route via N2 become installable. The recovery chain
therefore comprises the three stages of Table~\ref{tab:conv-breakdown}.

\vspace{0.4cm}
\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Breakdown of the convergence time after a direct-link failure}
\label{tab:conv-breakdown}
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
\textbf{Stage} & \textbf{Duration} \\
\midrule

\vspace{0.5cm}

The breakdown shows that the route computation itself is negligible
(microseconds on a three-node graph): the convergence time is set almost
entirely by the $2.0$\,s aging timeout, plus a sub-second tail for the relay
path to be established and the first forwarded packet to arrive. The most
direct way to accelerate convergence is therefore to reduce \texttt{MAX\_AGE},
at the cost of a higher risk of declaring a transient beacon loss as a
permanent failure; the chosen value of $2.0$\,s is a deliberate trade-off
between recovery speed and robustness to sporadic beacon loss in the shared
collision domain.

\subsection{Packet Inter-Arrival Timing}
\label{subsec:res-ia}

To verify that the relay preserves the temporal structure of the video stream,
the inter-arrival times of consecutive video packets were extracted from the
captures over a 30\,s window, for both the direct and relay conditions.
Figure~\ref{fig:ia} shows the two histograms side by side.

\vspace{0.4cm}
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{ia_timing.png}
\caption{Inter-arrival histograms of video packets at N3, direct link (left)
and relay path (right), over 30\,s. Both distributions are strongly bimodal
and practically identical: a dominant peak at $<10$\,ms (packets transmitted
back-to-back within one TDMA burst) and a second peak at $\approx

\label{fig:ia}
\end{figure}
\vspace{0.5cm}

The distribution has two peaks: $\approx 88\,\%$ of inter-arrivals fall below
10\,ms (packets within the same burst) and $\approx 11\,\%$ fall in the
130--150\,ms range (the inter-burst gap of one frame). This arises directly
from the schedule: N1 accumulates encoded data for $\approx 100$\,ms and then
transmits a burst during its 50\,ms slot. At 558\,kbps with 1316-byte MPEG-TS
UDP packets, each frame's worth of data is
\begin{equation}
n_{\mathrm{pkts}} = \frac{558{,}000 \times 0.150}{8 \times 1316} \approx 8\,\text{packets},
\label{eq:pkts-per-slot}
\end{equation}
consistent with the observed burst size of 8--9 packets.

indistinguishable from the direct one:
the burst fraction and the inter-burst gap are identical, and only the rare
tail differs (Table~\ref{tab:ia}). The relay forwards each packet within the
same slot in which it arrives, preserving the TDMA burst structure of the
stream. This is the streaming-data counterpart of the beacon analysis below:
it shows the schedule being respected by continuous high-rate traffic, not
just by the periodic control beacons.

\subsection{TDMA Slot Synchronisation}
\label{subsec:res-slots}

The previous subsections show that the end-to-end timing is consistent with
the schedule. This subsection proves the schedule directly, by examining the
instant at which each node transmits. The video stream is generated only by
N1, and therefore evidences only N1's slot; the synchronisation beacons,
however, are emitted by all three nodes and reveal the complete slot
structure. The beacons captured at N3 were wrapped to the 150\,ms frame and
attributed to their source node; Figure~\ref{fig:slots} shows the result over
a sequence of rounds.

\vspace{0.4cm}
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{slot_occupancy.png}
\caption{Beacon transmission instant wrapped to the TDMA frame, per round, for
the three nodes. Each node occupies its own slot in the strict cyclic order
N1\,$\to$\,N2\,$\to$\,N3, separated from the next by approximately one slot
(50\,ms), and remains in its slot across rounds. This is the direct evidence
that the nodes respect the TDMA schedule.}
\label{fig:slots}
\end{figure}
\vspace{0.5cm}

The three nodes occupy three disjoint regions in the cyclic order
N1\,$\to$\,N2\,$\to$\,N3, each separated from the next by approximately one
slot, and each remains in its region across rounds. After removing the common
frame rotation, the per-node standard deviation of the transmission instant is
$\approx 16$\,ms and 84--89\,\% of beacons fall within the assigned slot. Two
characteristics of the synchroniser should be noted when reading the figure.
First, the absolute phase of the frame is arbitrary: with no global clock,
only the relative ordering and spacing of the nodes carry meaning. Second, the
synchroniser advances all slots by a small common amount each round, so the
whole frame rotates slowly in wall-clock time ($\approx 18$\,ms/s); this
rotation is removed before evaluating the clustering, and over a short window
of a few rounds it is negligible and the slots appear stationary, as in
Figure~\ref{fig:slots}.

Under relay conditions the same analysis confirms that the cyclic ordering is
maintained. As noted in Section~\ref{subsec:linkfail}, with the direct link
blocked N3's application no longer receives N1's beacons (they are discarded by
\texttt{iptables} before the socket), so N3 synchronises through the two-hop
chain N1\,$\to$\,N2\,$\to$\,N3; the capture still records N1's beacons because
it taps the interface before the firewall. The slots remain occupied and
ordered, the only effect being a slightly looser alignment of N3 relative to
N1, consistent with N3 synchronising indirectly through N2.

Together, the inter-arrival analysis (streaming data, N1's slot) and this
beacon analysis (all three slots) establish the central claim of this
evaluation: the nodes respect their TDMA slots, for both periodic control
traffic and continuous video, and this property is preserved under relaying.

\section{Comparison with the Layer~2 Approach}
\label{sec:comparison}

The system evaluated here implements routing at Layer~3 (IP), using kernel
forwarding and standard routing tables over a TUN overlay. The prior work of
Morais~\cite{morais2024synchronization} implements routing at Layer~2, by
manipulating ARP tables so that the relay forwards frames transparently below
IP. Both run on the same RA-TDMAs+ synchroniser and the same class of
hardware. Table~\ref{tab:comparison} summarises the comparison; latency
figures are normalised to the frame period to account for the different frame
durations (100\,ms with four nodes in the reference, 150\,ms with three nodes
here).

\centering

routing approaches}

\begin{tabular}{lll}

Forwarding mechanism & ARP-table manipulation & IP forwarding (TUN + routes) \\

Slot compliance      & verified (beacons)    & verified (beacons + video) \\
Path observability   & invisible at IP layer & visible (\texttt{ip route}) \\

\bottomrule

The two approaches achieve equivalent TDMA-level behaviour: the normalised RTT
(0.9 vs.\ 0.88 frame), the 100\,\% PDR, and the verified slot compliance are
essentially the same, as expected since both share the synchroniser and the
slot structure. The differences are architectural.

\textbf{Latency distribution.} The reference reports a spread, triangular RTT
distribution, whereas this work observes a clean bimodal distribution. Both
are manifestations of the same slot-waiting mechanism; the bimodal shape
arises here because, with three nodes and the requester/responder slots
adjacent across the frame boundary, the reply either catches the next slot or
misses it by exactly one frame, producing two discrete levels rather than a
continuum.

\textbf{Convergence.} In both designs the recovery time after a link failure is
dominated by the RA-TDMAs+ node-aging timeout (\texttt{MAX\_AGE} $= 2.0$\,s),
i.e.\ the time required to detect that the direct link is dead before any
re-routing can begin (Section~\ref{subsec:res-convergence}). The two approaches
are therefore expected to recover in comparable time. The difference lies only
in the post-detection step, which is sub-second in both cases: the Layer~3
design re-installs the IP route and re-establishes the forwarding state,
whereas the Layer~2 design updates the ARP tables below IP. Reducing
\texttt{MAX\_AGE} is the most effective way to accelerate recovery in either
design, at the cost of robustness to transient beacon loss in the shared
collision domain.

\textbf{Observability and stability.} The Layer~3 approach trades convergence
speed for two practical advantages. The relay path is visible to standard IP
tools, which simplifies debugging and operation, whereas the Layer~2 path is
invisible at the IP layer. And, in contrast to the runtime instability
reported for the Layer~2 implementation, the Layer~3 system completed every
experiment without failure, benefiting from mature kernel forwarding rather
than ARP manipulation.

\subsection{Side-by-Side Re-implementation of the Layer~2 Method}
\label{subsec:l2-rerun}

The comparison above relies on the figures reported by Morais, obtained on a
different testbed at a different time. To place the comparison on a firmer
footing, the Layer~2 method was re-implemented and run on the same three-node
testbed used throughout this chapter, under the same conditions, so that the two
approaches could be measured directly against each other rather than across
separate experiments. The re-implementation follows the reference faithfully, in
the terms its report sets out: forwarding is performed at the data link layer by
installing static ARP entries that map each destination's address to the
next-hop MAC through \texttt{ioctl(SIOCSARP)}; the relay is a plain kernel
\texttt{ip\_forward} of the unmodified frame, with the source and destination
addresses left intact end to end; the video is carried over UDP; and the routing
module builds the same binary spanning tree from the connectivity matrix. The
decisive point for a fair comparison is that both methods run on the identical
RA-TDMAs+ synchroniser and the identical TDMA-agnostic framework, and differ
only in the routing and forwarding layer, so any difference that appears is
attributable to that layer alone and not to the platform, the schedule, or the
measurement tooling. The encoder was kept at the same 500\,kbps configuration
used for the Layer~3 results, and the node-aging timeout at the same
\texttt{MAX\_AGE} $= 2.0$\,s, to keep the comparison equal for equal.

\textbf{Convergence.} The convergence time, measured exactly as in
Section~\ref{subsec:res-convergence} (the gap in the delivered video between the
last packet over the direct path and the first packet forwarded through N2), is
reported in Table~\ref{tab:l2-conv}. The re-implemented Layer~2 method recovers
in a median of $\approx 2.25$\,s, against the $\approx 2.8$\,s of the Layer~3
system, a difference of roughly half a second, and with a far tighter spread:
the three Layer~2 runs fall within 4\,ms of one another, against a spread of
nearly 200\,ms for Layer~3. This is precisely the outcome anticipated in
Section~\ref{subsec:res-convergence}. Both methods pay the same $2.0$\,s
detection cost, because both detect the dead link through the same node-aging
timeout; what separates them is the data-path resumption that follows detection.
The Layer~2 path is connectionless, so once the ARP entry points at N2 the very
next frame is relayed by the kernel and the stream resumes almost immediately,
leaving only a short tail of about a quarter of a second. The Layer~3 path must
instead bring its connection-oriented TCP data plane back into service, and that
resumption is the $\approx 0.8$\,s tail isolated earlier. The measured gap of
$\approx 0.5$\,s between the two totals is therefore the empirical price of the
TCP data plane, and it confirms, on the same hardware, the architectural
analysis that had previously been argued only from the reference's reported
numbers.

\textbf{Streaming stability.} The convergence figure tells only half of the
story, because it marks when the first relayed packet arrives, not whether the
stream that follows is usable. Here the re-implementation exposed a genuine
difference. After recovery, the relayed video did not settle into a smooth flow
on our testbed: it arrived in bursts roughly one node-aging period apart, with
the topology repeatedly losing and regaining its direct neighbours, so that the
picture froze and jumped rather than playing continuously. Reducing the encoder
bitrate did not remove the effect, and that is the key observation, because it
shows the cause is not a shortage of raw bandwidth.

The mechanism is the interaction between the Layer~2 relay and the shared
collision domain. The relay is a kernel \texttt{ip\_forward} that is not
slot-aware: the instant N2 receives a video frame from N1 it re-transmits it,
which happens inside N1's slot rather than within a slot of N2's own. With the
three nodes in mutual range on one channel, these out-of-slot relay
transmissions contend with, and collide with, the synchronisation beacons, and
the resulting beacon loss stresses the RA-TDMAs+ synchroniser. This coupling is
not a surprise: Morais herself documents it, noting that forwarding delays at
the kernel level can place stress on the network and end up ``influencing the
synchronization protocol''. In our conditions the coupling was strong enough to
push neighbours past the $2.0$\,s aging timeout in bursts, so the route
oscillated between direct and relayed and the video was delivered in the
periodic bursts described above. The effect is therefore a property of the
shared medium and the non-slotted relay, not of the offered bitrate, which is
why lowering the rate did not cure it.

Two factors specific to our setup make this more severe than in the reference.
First, the failure is induced with \texttt{iptables} rather than by distance, so
N1 keeps transmitting on the channel even after it has been logically removed,
and its transmissions continue to contend for the medium on which N3 is trying
to receive N2; a genuine out-of-range failure would have silenced N1 and freed
the channel. Second, the testbed places all three nodes within mutual range, the
very condition Morais identifies as worst for collisions when she observes that
an ideal testbed would contain nodes that cannot hear one another, and that at
multi-hop ``the entire network becomes congested''. The Layer~3 system did not
show the same instability under identical conditions, which we attribute to its
TCP data plane recovering the packets lost to these collisions and to its
slot-gated source, though part of the difference may also reflect the particular
run conditions. We report the instability plainly, as an observed limitation of
the re-implementation on this testbed rather than a fundamental verdict on the
Layer~2 method, which the reference shows to function; separating how much is
intrinsic to the design and how much is an artefact of the shared-domain testbed
and the software-induced failure is left as future work.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:eval-summary}

This chapter evaluated the Layer~3 routing system across five properties, each
in its own subsection. The control channel exhibits a bimodal RTT whose two
clusters are separated by exactly one TDMA frame and whose mean of
$\approx 0.9$ frame matches the reference work, with 100\,\% PDR; the
quantisation and the absence of loss are positive evidence that the slots are
respected. Video throughput under relaying is within $\approx 1\,\%$ of the
direct baseline, and the inter-arrival distribution is identical in both
conditions, confirming that kernel forwarding at the relay preserves the TDMA
burst structure of the stream. The beacon analysis shows the three nodes
occupying their slots in strict cyclic order, both directly and through the
relay, establishing the central claim that the nodes respect their TDMA slots
for periodic control traffic and for continuous video alike. Routing
convergence after a link failure has a median of $\approx 2.8$\,s, dominated by
the $2.0$\,s node-aging timeout (\texttt{MAX\_AGE}) used to detect the dead
link, not by the routing computation, which completes in microseconds. The
comparison with the Layer~2 approach of Morais~\cite{morais2024synchronization}
was put on a first-hand footing by re-implementing that method and running it on
the same testbed (Section~\ref{subsec:l2-rerun}). The re-run confirms equivalent
TDMA-level performance and, as predicted, a faster convergence for the
connectionless Layer~2 data path ($\approx 2.25$\,s against $\approx 2.8$\,s, the
$\approx 0.5$\,s difference being the cost of the Layer~3 TCP resumption), while
also exposing, in the opposite direction, that the non-slotted Layer~2 relay
delivers an unstable, bursty stream in this single-collision-domain testbed,
where the Layer~3 system remained stable. Reducing the convergence time, and
separating how much of the Layer~2 streaming instability is intrinsic from how
much is an artefact of the shared-domain testbed, are identified as the main
directions for future work.
