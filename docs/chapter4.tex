\chapter{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{chap:evaluation}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:eval-intro}

This chapter presents the experimental evaluation of the Layer~3 routing
system described in Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}. The evaluation was
carried out on physical hardware in a three-node testbed that reproduces the
target operational scenario: a mobile robot transmitting a live video stream
to a stationary base station, with an intermediate relay node available to
maintain connectivity after a direct-link failure.

The evaluation pursues two objectives. The first is to characterise the
end-to-end performance of the system: control-channel latency, video
throughput, and recovery time after a link failure. The second, and central
to this dissertation, is to \emph{prove that the nodes respect their TDMA
slots} when transporting both periodic synchronisation beacons and a
continuous video stream, and that this property is preserved when traffic is
forwarded through the relay.

The chapter is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:setup} describes the
hardware, the network and software configuration, the video pipeline, and the
link-failure methodology. Section~\ref{sec:metrics} defines the performance
metrics. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents and analyses each result in a
dedicated subsection. Section~\ref{sec:comparison} compares the system with
the Layer~2 approach of Morais~\cite{morais2024synchronization}.
Section~\ref{sec:eval-summary} summarises the findings.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Testbed Overview}
\label{subsec:overview}

The testbed comprises three nodes, each with a fixed role. The network is an
IEEE~802.11 ad-hoc (IBSS) cell on channel~6. Two addressing planes coexist:
a \emph{physical} plane (\texttt{172.20.10.0/28}) carried by the wireless
interface, and a \emph{mesh} plane (\texttt{10.0.0.0/24}) carried by a TUN
virtual interface, through which all application traffic is routed by the
TDMA framework. The TDMA schedule uses a frame of $T_{\mathrm{frame}} =
150$\,ms divided into three slots of $T_{\mathrm{slot}} = 50$\,ms, one per
node, with a 5\,ms guard interval at the end of each slot. Slot~$i$ is
assigned to node~$i$, giving the fixed ordering
$[\text{N1}, \text{N2}, \text{N3}]$ shown in Figure~\ref{fig:frame}.

\vspace{0.4cm}
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\fbox{\parbox{0.95\textwidth}{\centering\vspace{1.4cm}
N1 slot $[0,50)$\,ms \qquad N2 slot $[50,100)$\,ms \qquad N3 slot $[100,150)$\,ms
\vspace{1.4cm}}}
\caption{TDMA frame structure with three synchronised nodes. The 150\,ms frame
is divided into three 50\,ms slots; each node transmits only within its own
slot.}
\label{fig:frame}
\end{figure}
\vspace{0.5cm}

All three nodes were placed on a laboratory bench within mutual radio range,
so that any pair could communicate directly. This is a deliberate constraint:
because the nodes share a single collision domain, link failures cannot be
produced by distance and must instead be induced in software
(Section~\ref{subsec:linkfail}). The same limitation was reported by
Morais~\cite{morais2024synchronization}.

Two distinct traffic planes travel over the air, and they must not be confused
when interpreting the captures:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Synchronisation beacons (control).} Each node broadcasts one
  beacon per frame, \emph{within its own slot}, to its neighbours as a small
  UDP packet (physical addresses, ports 7001--7003, $\approx 93$--$107$\,B).
  These carry the adjacency matrix and slot-timing information used by the
  RA-TDMAs+ synchroniser. They are \emph{not} relayed: every node emits its
  own.
  \item \textbf{Application data.} The video stream and the control channel
  travel over the mesh plane (\texttt{10.0.0.x}). The video is an MPEG-TS/UDP
  flow to \texttt{10.0.0.3:5000}; the teleoperation RTT probe is a UDP echo to
  \texttt{10.0.0.1:19876}. When the direct link is unavailable, this traffic
  is forwarded through N2.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Video Pipeline and Application Parameters}
\label{subsec:video-pipeline}

On N1, \texttt{rpicam-vid} captures raw YUV~4:2:0 frames and pipes them to
\texttt{ffmpeg}, which encodes them with \texttt{libx264}, wraps the output in
MPEG-TS, and sends each transport-stream segment as a UDP datagram to
\texttt{10.0.0.3:5000}. Because the destination is a mesh (TUN) address, the
datagram enters the TDMA routing layer transparently. It is worth noting the
two transport layers involved, because they matter for the throughput and
convergence analyses. The video application itself uses UDP, which is the
natural choice for a real-time stream; that UDP traffic is then carried between
the physical nodes by the TDMA framework, whose data plane is built on a TCP
connection over the wireless interface (the framework's periodic beacons, in
contrast, are sent over UDP). The relay therefore forwards the IP datagrams
below this framework transport, a distinction that becomes important in
Sections~\ref{subsec:res-throughput} and~\ref{subsec:res-convergence}. On N3,
\texttt{base\_station.py} receives each datagram, timestamps it, and forwards
it to \texttt{ffplay}. Table~\ref{tab:app-params} lists the parameters.

\subsection{Link-Failure Methodology}
\label{subsec:linkfail}

Because all nodes share a collision domain, a direct-link failure is induced
logically rather than physically. At the moment of failure, the following
rules are applied on N3:

\begin{verbatim}
iptables -I INPUT  1 -s 172.20.10.1 -j DROP
iptables -I OUTPUT 1 -d 172.20.10.1 -j DROP
\end{verbatim}

These rules discard, at the IP layer, every frame to and from N1's physical
address, making the direct link invisible to N3's TDMA framework and to the
TCP data plane, and forcing traffic onto the relay path
N1\,$\to$\,N2\,$\to$\,N3. The rules are cleared between repetitions with
\texttt{iptables -F}.

To confirm that the block was effective before each relay measurement, N3
attempted to reach N1's physical address with \texttt{ping}
(\texttt{172.20.10.1}); the physical address, unlike the mesh address, is not
reachable through the relay, so a failing ping confirms that the direct link was
genuinely severed and that any subsequent traffic must have travelled through
N2.

\section{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

This section defines the metrics used throughout the evaluation. They are the
standard quantities used to characterise a packet network, adapted where
necessary to the TDMA context in which each leg of a transmission can only
depart within its node's slot.

\subsection{Round-Trip Time (RTT)}
\label{subsec:m-rtt}

The round-trip time is the elapsed time between N3 sending a UDP echo request to
N1 and receiving the corresponding reply. For the $k$-th probe it is given by
Equation~\ref{eq:rtt}, where $t^{\mathrm{tx}}_k$ is the instant the request is
sent and $t^{\mathrm{rx}}_k$ the instant the reply is received, both timestamped
at N3.

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RTT}_k = t^{\mathrm{rx}}_k - t^{\mathrm{tx}}_k
\label{eq:rtt}
\end{equation}

In a TDMA network the RTT reflects the slot structure directly, since each leg
of the round trip can leave its node only during that node's slot.

\subsection{Packet Delivery Ratio (PDR)}
\label{subsec:m-pdr}

The packet delivery ratio is the fraction of echo requests that are answered
within a timeout, expressed as a percentage in Equation~\ref{eq:pdr}, where
$N_{\mathrm{rx}}$ is the number of replies received and $N_{\mathrm{tx}}$ the
number of requests sent. It is the complement of the packet loss ratio and
measures the reliability of the path.

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PDR} = \frac{N_{\mathrm{rx}}}{N_{\mathrm{tx}}} \times 100\,\%
\label{eq:pdr}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Jitter}
\label{subsec:m-jitter}

Jitter quantifies the variability of the latency, which is critical for
real-time applications such as a live video link. The average relative jitter
used here is the mean absolute difference between consecutive RTT samples,
defined in Equation~\ref{eq:jitter} over $N$ measurements for a given pair of
nodes.

\begin{equation}
J = \frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{k=2}^{N}\bigl\lvert \mathrm{RTT}_k - \mathrm{RTT}_{k-1}\bigr\rvert
\label{eq:jitter}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Throughput}
\label{subsec:m-throughput}

Throughput is the rate at which application data is successfully delivered to
the destination over an observation window. It is computed from the delivered
video payload as in Equation~\ref{eq:throughput}, where $B_{\mathrm{total}}$ is
the sum of the video payload bytes received at N3 during a window of duration
$\Delta t$, and the factor of eight converts bytes to bits.

\begin{equation}
T = \frac{8\,B_{\mathrm{total}}}{\Delta t} \quad [\text{kbps}]
\label{eq:throughput}
\end{equation}

The video application emits an MPEG-TS stream over UDP into the mesh interface;
this traffic is then carried between physical nodes by the TDMA framework, whose
data plane uses a TCP connection (the framework's beacons, by contrast, use
UDP). Throughput is measured at the application level --- the delivered video
payload --- so that the direct and relay conditions count the same quantity
regardless of how the framework encapsulates it on the wire
(Section~\ref{subsec:res-throughput}).

\subsection{Convergence Time}
\label{subsec:m-conv}

The convergence time is the interval during which the video stream is
interrupted while the system re-routes through the relay after the direct link
fails. It is measured as in Equation~\ref{eq:tconv-m}, the gap between the last
video packet delivered over the direct path and the first packet recovered
through N2.

\begin{equation}
t_{\mathrm{conv}} = t_{\mathrm{first\,relay}} - t_{\mathrm{last\,direct}}
\label{eq:tconv-m}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Inter-Arrival Time (IA)}
\label{subsec:m-ia}

The inter-arrival time is the interval between two consecutive video packets
arriving at N3, defined in Equation~\ref{eq:ia}. Its distribution reveals the
TDMA burst structure of the stream and allows verification that relay forwarding
preserves it.

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IA}_k = t_{k+1} - t_k
\label{eq:ia}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Slot Occupancy}
\label{subsec:m-slot}

To verify that each node transmits only within its assigned slot, the
transmission instant of every captured beacon is wrapped to the frame period, as
in Equation~\ref{eq:phi}, and attributed to its source node.

\begin{equation}
\phi = t \bmod T_{\mathrm{frame}}
\label{eq:phi}
\end{equation}

A node respects its slot if its beacons cluster within its assigned 50\,ms
interval. Because the RA-TDMAs+ synchroniser advances all slots by a common
amount on every round, the whole frame rotates slowly in wall-clock time; this
common rotation is removed before the per-node clustering is evaluated, leaving
the relative synchronisation between the nodes.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

This section is organised so that each subsection isolates one property of the
system: it states briefly how the corresponding test was performed, presents
the figure that supports the result, and analyses it immediately afterwards.

\subsection{Control-Channel Latency}
\label{subsec:res-rtt}

To characterise control-plane latency, N3 sent 100 UDP echo requests to
\texttt{10.0.0.1} at 10\,Hz, with N1 replying to each immediately; the 100
round-trip samples were recorded at N3. The test was performed over the direct
link and over the relay path. Figure~\ref{fig:rtt-direct} shows the result for
the direct link.

\vspace{0.4cm}
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{RTT_over_the_direct_link.png}
\caption{Control-channel RTT over the direct link (N3\,$\to$\,N1, 100 pings,
PDR $= 100\,\%$). \textbf{Left:} RTT per ping sequence; the values alternate
in blocks between a low cluster ($\approx 50$\,ms, blue) and a high cluster
($\approx 200$\,ms, red). \textbf{Right:} the resulting distribution is
strongly bimodal, with the two clusters separated by exactly one TDMA frame
(150\,ms) and an empty band in between --- the signature of TDMA round
quantisation.}
\label{fig:rtt-direct}
\end{figure}
\vspace{0.5cm}

The RTT does not concentrate around a single value; it splits into two
well-separated clusters with an empty band between roughly 60 and 190\,ms.
This is a direct consequence of the slot structure, and it is best understood
by following a single probe through the schedule. The round trip is made of
two independent legs, and each leg can leave its node only during that node's
50\,ms slot. When N3 wishes to send the echo request it cannot transmit
immediately: it must wait until N3's slot comes round, so the request is held
in the transmit queue for up to one frame before it even enters the medium.
Once the request reaches N1, the reply faces the symmetric constraint --- it
can depart only inside N1's slot. The total RTT is therefore the sum of the
data-plane transmission time, the processing at each end, and, dominating all
of these, the two quantised waits for the respective slots. Because a wait for
a slot is, by construction, a wait for a position in a periodic 150\,ms frame,
the RTT is built up from whole-frame and fractional-frame components rather
than from a smoothly varying delay.

The two clusters correspond to the two possible outcomes of the second, decisive
slot wait. After the request arrives, the reply either reaches N1's transmit
opportunity in the immediately following frame --- producing the lower cluster
at $\approx 52$\,ms, a value fixed by the data-plane encapsulation and the
once-per-frame transmission overhead rather than by any variable queueing ---
or it misses that opportunity by a small margin and must wait one entire frame
for the next one, producing the upper cluster at $\approx 52 + 150 = 202$\,ms.
There is no intermediate outcome: a leg cannot wait ``half a slot extra'',
because the next transmit opportunity is always exactly one frame away. The
cluster separation of precisely one frame (150\,ms), and the empty band between
the clusters, are therefore the signature of TDMA round quantisation --- latency
is gained or lost only in whole-frame units. This is the point at which the
present results differ in \emph{shape} from the reference: Morais reports a
broadly triangular RTT histogram, a continuum of values filling the range,
whereas here the histogram is cleanly two-valued. Both shapes arise from the
same slot-waiting mechanism, but the geometry differs. In the reference, with
four nodes and the probe and reply slots not adjacent, the residual wait is
distributed almost uniformly across a round and fills the histogram into a
triangle; here, with three nodes and the requester and responder slots adjacent
across the frame boundary, the reply either just catches the next slot or just
misses it, collapsing the continuum into two discrete levels.

The fraction of pings that lands in each cluster is not fixed: it changes from
run to run, and even drifts slowly within a single run. This is not noise but a
deterministic beat. The probe is emitted by a free-running 10\,Hz timer, i.e.\
once every 100\,ms, while the schedule repeats every 150\,ms; because these two
periods are incommensurate, the phase at which successive probes meet N3's slot
walks steadily through the frame, so a probe that catches its slot now will,
several pings later, just miss it, and the samples migrate between the two
clusters in slow blocks --- exactly the block structure visible in the
left-hand time series of Figure~\ref{fig:rtt-direct}. A consequence is that the
arithmetic mean and the median of the raw RTT are not meaningful descriptors:
their value depends only on the accidental ratio of fast to slow samples in a
given capture, and a number such as ``a mean of 130\,ms'' describes neither
cluster and would not be reproduced by a second run. For this reason the two
clusters and their separation are reported separately in
Table~\ref{tab:rtt}, rather than a single summary statistic.

The same test over the relay path is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rtt-relay}.

\vspace{0.4cm}
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{RTT_over_the_relay_path.png}
\caption{Control-channel RTT over the relay path
(N3\,$\to$\,N2\,$\to$\,N1, 100 pings, PDR $= 100\,\%$). The distribution is
practically identical to the direct link in Figure~\ref{fig:rtt-direct}: the
same two clusters, the same one-frame separation. Relaying therefore adds no
significant latency to the control channel.}
\label{fig:rtt-relay}
\end{figure}
\vspace{0.5cm}

The relay distribution is practically identical to the direct one: the same
two clusters, in the same positions, with the same one-frame separation. This
is a stronger statement than it may appear. Inserting N2 into the path could,
in principle, have added a third slot wait --- the forwarded packet waiting for
N2's own transmit opportunity --- which would have shifted the whole
distribution up by one slot or introduced a third cluster. No such shift is
observed. The explanation is the same as on the data plane: N2 forwards the
control packet through the kernel routing engine within the handling of the
slot in which it arrives, rather than re-queueing it as fresh application
traffic for N2's next slot, so the extra hop costs only a few milliseconds of
processing rather than a full slot of scheduling latency. The control-plane and
data-plane results are thus two views of the same property: the relay is
transparent because forwarding happens below the application and does not
consume a separate scheduling step.

It is worth stating explicitly why this RTT behaviour supports, rather than
merely fails to contradict, the central claim that the nodes respect their
slots. If the nodes were \emph{not} synchronised --- if slots overlapped or
drifted unboundedly --- the medium would suffer collisions and contention, and
the resulting RTT would be unstructured: a long, ragged tail of values with no
preferred levels, accompanied by packet loss from collided transmissions.
What is observed is the opposite. The latency is confined to two sharp levels
separated by exactly one frame, the maximum stays below two frames, and the
packet delivery ratio is $100\,\%$ in both the direct and relay conditions:
every one of the 100 probes was answered. A network can only produce
quantisation this clean, with no loss, if each node is in fact transmitting
within a well-defined slot and the slots are correctly ordered; the structure
of the RTT is therefore a direct, if indirect-looking, measurement of slot
compliance. Normalising by the frame period, the mean RTT of $\approx 0.9$
frame matches the value reported by Morais~\cite{morais2024synchronization}
(0.88 frame): although the histogram shape differs, the underlying
TDMA-level latency --- a round trip costing a little under one frame on average
--- is the same, as expected from a shared synchroniser and slot structure.

\subsection{Video Throughput}
\label{subsec:res-throughput}

Throughput is the rate at which video payload is \emph{delivered} to the base
station, and it was measured in a way that is both non-intrusive and fair to
compare between the direct and relay conditions. The video stream produced by
N1 was left running continuously, and a passive capture at N3 observed the
flow for a 15\,s window, recording the size and arrival instant of every video
datagram. The capture is passive in a strict sense: it attaches a raw
\texttt{AF\_PACKET} socket to the interface and reads a copy of each frame,
rather than binding UDP port~5000. This distinction matters. Had the
measurement tool bound port~5000 it would either have collided with the
\texttt{ffplay} receiver (the operating system does not allow two ordinary
sockets to own the same port) or, worse, silently stolen datagrams from the
player and distorted the very quantity being measured. By reading a copy below
the socket layer, the tool coexists with the live player and observes exactly
the bytes the application receives, without perturbing the stream. The
experiment was repeated three times over the direct link and three times over
the relay path, and the results were averaged to suppress the run-to-run
variation that the variable-bitrate encoder introduces as the scene changes.

The quantity reported here is the \emph{application-level} throughput: the
MPEG-TS video payload delivered end-to-end from N1 to N3, identified by the
mesh flow \texttt{10.0.0.1}\,$\to$\,\texttt{10.0.0.3:5000}. It deliberately
excludes the wire-level framing, and this choice is what makes the direct and
relay numbers comparable. On the direct path the video is carried inside the
TDMA framework's own transport encapsulation, whereas on the relay path N2
forwards the raw IP datagrams; counting the encapsulated wire bytes would
therefore measure two different things and would wrongly suggest that the relay
halves the throughput. Counting the delivered video payload instead measures
the same thing in both cases --- the bytes the player actually consumes --- and
isolates the effect of the relay hop from the framing overhead. A useful
by-product of inspecting the relay capture is direct evidence that the
forwarding is transparent: the relayed video appears on the wire as plain
MPEG-TS/UDP datagrams addressed \texttt{10.0.0.1}\,$\to$\,\texttt{10.0.0.3},
\emph{not} re-wrapped in any additional framework header. The relay is thus a
genuine network-layer forward (the kernel routing engine moving an IP packet
from one interface to the other), not a re-origination of the stream.
Figure~\ref{fig:throughput} and Table~\ref{tab:throughput} report the means.

The direct-link mean of 558\,kbps sits about 12\,\% above the 500\,kbps figure
configured in the encoder. This is expected, and decomposing it is instructive
because the gap is structural rather than a measurement artefact. The 500\,kbps
target governs the \emph{compressed video elementary stream} that
\texttt{libx264} emits; it does not include the cost of packaging that stream
for transport. Two mechanisms add the remaining bytes.

First, the MPEG-TS container imposes a fixed framing tax. The elementary stream
is segmented into 188-byte transport packets, each carrying a 4-byte header, so
the container alone inflates the payload by $4/184 \approx 2.2\,\%$ before it
ever reaches the network; the UDP datagrams then group seven such transport
packets ($7 \times 188 = 1316$\,B) and, once the UDP (8\,B) and IP (20\,B)
headers are added, the on-the-wire framing accounts for a further few per cent.
Together the containerisation and headers explain the bulk of the excess.

Second, and more significantly, the encoder is configured with \texttt{-g~1},
which forces every frame to be coded as an independent intra (I) frame and
disables inter-frame prediction entirely. This choice is deliberate: with an
all-intra stream the loss of any single packet damages only one frame and the
video self-heals on the next frame, which is the behaviour wanted for a
low-latency teleoperation link over a lossy wireless medium. The cost is rate:
without P- or B-frames the encoder cannot exploit temporal redundancy between
successive frames, so each frame is encoded from scratch and the rate
controller produces a higher and far more constant bitrate than an equivalent
GOP-structured stream would. The 558\,kbps observed is therefore the natural
operating point of a robustness-oriented, all-intra configuration, not a
malfunction of the rate control.

A 558\,kbps stream is comfortably within the capacity that the schedule grants
to N1, which is the reason the stream is sustained without loss despite being
confined to one slot in three. N1 may transmit only during its 50\,ms slot of
every 150\,ms frame, i.e.\ for one third of wall-clock time. To deliver a
mean of 558\,kbps while transmitting for only a third of the time, the
\emph{instantaneous} rate inside the slot must be roughly three times the mean,
about 1.7\,Mbps. This is a small fraction of the raw rate available on the
IEEE~802.11 channel, so the slot has ample headroom: the burst of $\approx 8$
datagrams that constitutes one frame's worth of video (derived in
Equation~\ref{eq:pkts-per-slot}, Section~\ref{subsec:res-ia}) drains well
within the 50\,ms slot, leaving the guard interval clear. This headroom is what
allows the stream to coexist with the periodic beacons and the teleoperation
control traffic inside the same slot structure without any of them being
starved, and it is the quantitative reason the schedule is never the limiting
factor on the video plane.

Under relay conditions the mean throughput is 552\,kbps, a reduction of only
$\approx 1\,\%$ relative to the direct path. This difference is smaller than
the run-to-run variation the variable-bitrate encoder produces as the scene
content changes, so it cannot be attributed to the relay hop; no systematic
degradation is observed. The result is significant because, a priori, inserting
an intermediate node could have cost a full extra slot of latency per frame and
a corresponding reduction in sustained rate if N2 had to schedule the forwarded
video into its \emph{own} transmit opportunity. It does not. As established
above, N2 forwards the raw IP datagrams through the kernel routing engine
rather than re-originating them as fresh application traffic, so the video is
carried within the existing slot structure instead of consuming a separate
TDMA scheduling step at the relay. The relay is therefore effectively
\emph{transparent} on the video plane, and this is the data-plane counterpart
of the control-plane result of Section~\ref{subsec:res-rtt}, where forwarding
through N2 likewise added no measurable latency. The two observations reinforce
each other: the same forwarding mechanism that leaves the round-trip
distribution unchanged also leaves the delivered throughput unchanged.

At first sight this $\approx 1\,\%$ result appears to contradict the reference
work of Morais~\cite{morais2024synchronization}, which reported a severe
throughput reduction with relaying --- to roughly one half of the
single-hop value with one relay, and to about one fifth with two relays. The
apparent contradiction dissolves once the two measurements are recognised as
probing different regimes, and the contrast is itself informative.

The reduction reported in the reference is the classic capacity collapse of a
single-channel multi-hop wireless chain. When every node shares one radio
channel and the medium is half-duplex, a packet forwarded by a relay must be
\emph{received} and then \emph{re-transmitted} on the same channel; the relay's
two transmissions, plus those of the source, contend for one shared resource,
so the maximum end-to-end rate falls roughly as $C/n$ with the number of hops
$n$. Crucially, that ceiling is only \emph{reached}, and therefore only
\emph{measured}, by a flow that deliberately saturates the link. The reference
obtained its figures with \texttt{iperf3}, whose explicit purpose is to push as
much TCP traffic as the medium will accept until congestion and retransmissions
cap the rate; under that load, and under CSMA/CA with no slotting, the per-hop
halving is exactly what should appear.

The measurement here is of a fundamentally different kind. The video is a
\emph{fixed-rate, non-saturating} application stream of $\approx 558$\,kbps,
which is between one and two per cent of the per-hop capacity --- even after a
single relay hop has halved that capacity, several megabits per second of
headroom remain. The quantity of interest is therefore not the link's maximum
capacity but whether the offered rate is \emph{preserved} end to end, and at a
load this far below the ceiling the second hop forwards the stream without ever
throttling it. The bottleneck that produces the collapse binds only as the
offered load approaches capacity; our application never operates in that
regime. Two further factors widen the gap: the TDMA schedule eliminates the
CSMA/CA collisions and retransmissions that account for much of the reference's
loss, and, as established above, the kernel forward at N2 does not consume a
separate TDMA slot. In the interest of intellectual honesty, a saturating
\texttt{iperf3} test over our relay path would still be expected to exhibit a
per-hop reduction, for the same capacity-sharing reason; the point is precisely
that the teleoperation video does not, and is never intended to, operate at
saturation. For the application that this system is built to carry, the
operationally relevant regime is the source-limited one, and in that regime the
relay is transparent.

The 558/552\,kbps figures are time-averages, but the instantaneous delivery is
far from flat: because N1 may transmit only inside its slot, the video arrives
in bursts separated by silent inter-burst gaps of one frame, and the mean
throughput is the integral of this bursty profile over the observation window.
This temporal structure is exactly what the inter-arrival analysis of
Section~\ref{subsec:res-ia} quantifies --- roughly 88\,\% of the datagrams
arrive back-to-back within a burst and the remainder are separated by a
one-frame gap --- and it is identical for the direct and relay conditions. The
flatness of the \emph{averaged} throughput over the window therefore does not
contradict the burstiness of the stream; it is the expected consequence of a
constant-rate encoder feeding a periodic slot, sampled over many frames.

\subsection{Routing Convergence}
\label{subsec:res-convergence}

Convergence is the time the system takes to restore the video stream through
the relay after the direct link fails. It was measured entirely at N3 from the
passive video capture, as follows. First, the direct-link stream was allowed
to stabilise for at least three seconds. The \texttt{iptables} rules of
Section~\ref{subsec:linkfail} were then applied to sever the direct link. The
capture was monitored for two events: the \emph{gap}, defined as the instant
no video packet had arrived for more than $0.4$\,s (its timestamp,
$t_{\mathrm{last\,direct}}$, marks the last packet delivered over the direct
path); and the \emph{recovery}, defined as the arrival of the first video
packet after the gap, forwarded through N2 ($t_{\mathrm{first\,relay}}$). The
convergence time is
\begin{equation}
t_{\mathrm{conv}} = t_{\mathrm{first\,relay}} - t_{\mathrm{last\,direct}}.
\label{eq:tconv}
\end{equation}
The direct link was then restored and the rules cleared before the next
repetition. Three repetitions were performed; Table~\ref{tab:convergence}
reports the results.

The median convergence time of the two repetitions that produced a measurable
gap is $\approx 2.8$\,s, equivalent to about 18~TDMA frames. The two
recovered values (2675 and 2863\,ms) are close, which already hints that the
delay is governed by a fixed timeout rather than by a variable process such as
contention or retransmission, whose spread would have been far wider. The third
repetition is qualitatively different and is reported separately: the relay
route was still installed in the routing tables from the previous repetition,
so when the direct link was cut the stream switched paths in less than the
$0.4$\,s detection threshold and no gap was recorded at all. Rather than being
discarded as a failed measurement, this case is itself informative --- it shows
that the cost of convergence is almost entirely the cost of \emph{detecting}
the failure, not of computing or installing the new route, because when
detection is bypassed (the route is already present) the failover is effectively
instantaneous. It is excluded from the median only because it does not measure
the same quantity as the other two.

The dominant component of the convergence time is the \emph{link-failure
detection}, which is governed by the node-aging mechanism of the RA-TDMAs+
adjacency matrix. Each node keeps, for every neighbour, the timestamp of the
last beacon received from it; the neighbour's \emph{age} is the elapsed time
since that beacon. On every round the function \texttt{removeDeadLinks()}
compares each age against the constant \texttt{MAX\_AGE}, and a neighbour whose
age reaches \texttt{MAX\_AGE} is declared dead and removed from the matrix. In
this implementation \texttt{MAX\_AGE} $= 2.0$\,s, so when the direct link to N1
is severed, N3 stops receiving N1's beacons but continues to treat N1 as a live
neighbour for a full $2.0$\,s before the link is removed. This is a deliberate
design feature, not an accident of timing: because the three nodes share one
collision domain, an individual beacon is occasionally lost to a transient
collision even when the link is perfectly healthy, and declaring a neighbour
dead on the first missed beacon would cause the topology to flap continuously.
The aging timeout is the hysteresis that prevents this --- it requires the
absence of beacons to persist for $2.0$\,s, i.e.\ for roughly thirteen
consecutive missed beacons at the 150\,ms frame, before believing the link is
truly gone. Only once N1 has aged out does the topology change and the route via
N2 become installable. The recovery chain therefore comprises the three stages
of Table~\ref{tab:conv-breakdown}.

The breakdown shows that the route computation itself is negligible
(microseconds on a three-node graph): the convergence time is set almost
entirely by the $2.0$\,s aging timeout, plus a sub-second tail for the relay
path to be established and the first forwarded packet to arrive. This
localisation of the cost has a clear practical consequence. Because detection
dominates and route installation is essentially free, the convergence time is
not a property of the routing layer's efficiency at all --- a faster routing
algorithm or a smaller graph would not change it --- but a property of a single
tunable constant. The most direct way to accelerate convergence is therefore to
reduce \texttt{MAX\_AGE}: halving it to $1.0$\,s would roughly halve the
recovery time, and the lower bound on detection is set only by how aggressively
one is willing to age out links. That aggressiveness is precisely the trade-off.
A shorter timeout recovers faster from genuine failures but is more likely to
mistake a transient burst of collisions for a dead link, tearing down and
rebuilding a healthy route and momentarily interrupting the very stream it is
meant to protect; a longer timeout is more robust to sporadic beacon loss but
slower to react. The chosen value of $2.0$\,s sits deliberately on the
conservative side of this trade-off, favouring stability of the topology in the
shared collision domain over raw recovery speed, which is the appropriate
choice for a teleoperation link where a spurious route flap is more disruptive
than a bounded, one-off recovery delay. Tuning this constant, or replacing the
fixed timeout with an adaptive scheme that reacts to the recent beacon-loss
rate, is identified as a concrete avenue for future work.

\subsection{TDMA Slot Synchronisation}
\label{subsec:res-slots}

The previous subsections show that the end-to-end timing is consistent with
the schedule. This subsection proves the schedule directly, by examining the
instant at which each node transmits. The video stream is generated only by
N1, and therefore evidences only N1's slot; the synchronisation beacons,
however, are emitted by all three nodes and reveal the complete slot
structure. The beacons captured at N3 were wrapped to the 150\,ms frame and
attributed to their source node; Figure~\ref{fig:slots} shows the result over
a sequence of rounds.

\vspace{0.4cm}
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{slot_occupancy.png}
\caption{Beacon transmission instant wrapped to the TDMA frame, per round, for
the three nodes. Each node occupies its own slot in the strict cyclic order
N1\,$\to$\,N2\,$\to$\,N3, separated from the next by approximately one slot
(50\,ms), and remains in its slot across rounds. This is the direct evidence
that the nodes respect the TDMA schedule.}
\label{fig:slots}
\end{figure}
\vspace{0.5cm}

The data underlying Figure~\ref{fig:slots} were obtained by capturing the
beacon traffic at N3 with a passive sniffer and recording, for each beacon, its
arrival instant and its source node (identifiable from the physical address and
the source port, 7001--7003). The capture taps the wireless interface directly,
below the firewall, which is important for two reasons. First, it sees every
beacon that reaches the antenna regardless of any \texttt{iptables} rule, so the
same method works unchanged in the direct and relay conditions. Second, it
observes the transmissions as they actually occur on the medium, which is what a
claim about slot occupancy must be about. This differs from the external
monitor-mode capture used in the reference work: rather than introducing a
fourth, passive observer node, the synchronisation is verified from the
perspective of an active participant, N3, which is sufficient because N3 is in
radio range of both other nodes and therefore receives all three beacons.

The three nodes occupy three disjoint regions in the cyclic order
N1\,$\to$\,N2\,$\to$\,N3, each separated from the next by approximately one
slot, and each remains in its region across rounds. Quantitatively, after the
processing described below, the per-node standard deviation of the transmission
instant is $\approx 16$\,ms --- small relative to the 50\,ms slot --- and
84--89\,\% of all beacons fall within their node's assigned slot, the remainder
lying in the 5\,ms guard band or spilling marginally into a neighbour's slot
under transient load. That the three clouds neither overlap nor exchange order
across the captured rounds is the direct, transmitter-side evidence that the
schedule is being respected: each node is in fact confining its transmissions
to its own slot, which is the property the whole evaluation set out to
establish.

Two characteristics of the RA-TDMAs+ synchroniser must be understood to read
the figure correctly, and both stem from the fact that the network has no
global clock. First, the absolute phase of the frame is arbitrary: there is no
external reference that fixes where ``time zero'' of the frame lies, so only the
\emph{relative} ordering and spacing of the nodes carry physical meaning. A plot
in which all three clouds were shifted bodily by the same amount would describe
an equally valid, equally synchronised network; it is the gaps between the
nodes, not their absolute positions, that matter. Second, the synchroniser does
not hold the frame fixed in wall-clock time. To keep the nodes aligned it
advances all slots by a small common amount on every round, with the effect that
the entire frame rotates slowly against wall-clock time, at $\approx 18$\,ms/s
in these captures. This common rotation is a property of all nodes together and
says nothing about their mutual alignment, so it is removed (the per-round
common offset is subtracted) before the clustering is evaluated; what remains is
the relative synchronisation between nodes, which is the quantity of interest.
Over the short window of a few rounds shown in Figure~\ref{fig:slots} the
rotation is small enough to be neglected and the slots appear stationary, but
over a longer capture it must be de-rotated explicitly or the clouds would
smear across the frame and obscure the very alignment they are meant to show.

Under relay conditions the same analysis confirms that the cyclic ordering is
maintained. This case requires care, because a naive reading of the capture
seems paradoxical: with the direct link to N1 blocked, how can N1's beacons
still appear at N3 and still fall in N1's slot? The resolution lies in where the
two operations act. The \texttt{iptables} rule discards N1's packets at the IP
layer, whereas the passive sniffer taps the wireless interface below it, before
the firewall. N1's beacons are therefore removed from N3's TDMA
\emph{application} --- which is why N3 stops treating N1 as a direct neighbour
and falls back to synchronising through the two-hop chain
N1\,$\to$\,N2\,$\to$\,N3 --- while still being \emph{recorded} by the capture,
because they continue to reach the antenna. The capture thus shows
the true state of the medium (N1 is still transmitting, still in its slot),
whereas the routing layer reflects the logical topology (N1 is unreachable
directly). Far from undermining the result, this confirms two things at once:
that N1 keeps its slot regardless of how N3 routes to it, and that the
link-failure abstraction is being imposed at the right layer. The only
observable effect of the relay condition is a slightly looser alignment of N3
relative to N1, which is expected, since N3 now derives its timing indirectly
through N2 rather than directly from N1 and therefore inherits one extra hop of
synchronisation slack.

Together, the inter-arrival analysis (streaming data, N1's slot) and this
beacon analysis (all three slots) establish the central claim of this
evaluation: the nodes respect their TDMA slots, for both periodic control
traffic and continuous video, and this property is preserved under relaying.

\section{Comparison with the Layer~2 Approach}
\label{sec:comparison}

The system evaluated here implements routing at Layer~3 (IP), using kernel
forwarding and standard routing tables over a TUN overlay. The prior work of
Morais~\cite{morais2024synchronization} implements routing at Layer~2, by
manipulating ARP tables so that the relay forwards frames transparently below
IP. Both run on the same RA-TDMAs+ synchroniser and the same class of
hardware. Table~\ref{tab:comparison} summarises the comparison; latency
figures are normalised to the frame period to account for the different frame
durations (100\,ms with four nodes in the reference, 150\,ms with three nodes
here).

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Comparison between the Layer~2 (reference) and Layer~3 (this work)
routing approaches}
\label{tab:comparison}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{Aspect} & \textbf{Layer~2 (Morais)} & \textbf{Layer~3 (this work)} \\
\midrule
Forwarding mechanism & ARP-table manipulation & IP forwarding (TUN + routes) \\
Routing layer        & Data link             & Network \\
Mean RTT (norm.)     & 0.88 frame            & $\approx 0.9$ frame \\
RTT distribution     & spread / triangular   & bimodal (frame-quantised) \\
PDR                  & 100\,\%               & 100\,\% \\
Slot compliance      & verified (beacons)    & verified (beacons + video) \\
Path observability   & invisible at IP layer & visible (\texttt{ip route}) \\
Convergence          & aging-limited ($\approx 2.8$\,s) & aging-limited ($\approx 2.8$\,s) \\
Reported stability   & runtime failures      & stable across all experiments \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The two approaches achieve equivalent TDMA-level behaviour: the normalised RTT
(0.9 vs.\ 0.88 frame), the 100\,\% PDR, and the verified slot compliance are
essentially the same, as expected since both share the synchroniser and the
slot structure. The differences are architectural.

\textbf{Latency distribution.} The reference reports a spread, triangular RTT
distribution, whereas this work observes a clean bimodal distribution. Both
are manifestations of the same slot-waiting mechanism; the bimodal shape
arises here because, with three nodes and the requester/responder slots
adjacent across the frame boundary, the reply either catches the next slot or
misses it by exactly one frame, producing two discrete levels rather than a
continuum.

\textbf{Convergence.} In both designs the recovery time after a link failure is
dominated by the RA-TDMAs+ node-aging timeout (\texttt{MAX\_AGE} $= 2.0$\,s),
i.e.\ the time required to detect that the direct link is dead before any
re-routing can begin (Section~\ref{subsec:res-convergence}). The two approaches
are therefore expected to recover in comparable time. The difference lies only
in the post-detection step, which is sub-second in both cases: the Layer~3
design re-installs the IP route and re-establishes the forwarding state,
whereas the Layer~2 design updates the ARP tables below IP. Reducing
\texttt{MAX\_AGE} is the most effective way to accelerate recovery in either
design, at the cost of robustness to transient beacon loss in the shared
collision domain.

\textbf{Observability and stability.} The Layer~3 approach trades convergence
speed for two practical advantages. The relay path is visible to standard IP
tools, which simplifies debugging and operation, whereas the Layer~2 path is
invisible at the IP layer. And, in contrast to the runtime instability
reported for the Layer~2 implementation, the Layer~3 system completed every
experiment without failure, benefiting from mature kernel forwarding rather
than ARP manipulation.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:eval-summary}

This chapter evaluated the Layer~3 routing system across five properties, each
in its own subsection. The control channel exhibits a bimodal RTT whose two
clusters are separated by exactly one TDMA frame and whose mean of
$\approx 0.9$ frame matches the reference work, with 100\,\% PDR; the
quantisation and the absence of loss are positive evidence that the slots are
respected. Video throughput under relaying is within $\approx 1\,\%$ of the
direct baseline, and the inter-arrival distribution is identical in both
conditions, confirming that kernel forwarding at the relay preserves the TDMA
burst structure of the stream. The beacon analysis shows the three nodes
occupying their slots in strict cyclic order, both directly and through the
relay, establishing the central claim that the nodes respect their TDMA slots
for periodic control traffic and for continuous video alike. Routing
convergence after a link failure has a median of $\approx 2.8$\,s, dominated by
the $2.0$\,s node-aging timeout (\texttt{MAX\_AGE}) used to detect the dead
link, not by the routing computation, which completes in microseconds. The
comparison with the Layer~2 approach of Morais~\cite{morais2024synchronization}
shows equivalent TDMA-level performance, comparable convergence (both governed
by the shared aging timeout), and better observability and stability for the
Layer~3 design developed here. Reducing the convergence time and completing a
side-by-side re-run of the Layer~2 system on the same testbed are identified
as the main directions for future work.
