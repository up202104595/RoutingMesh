\chapter{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{chap:evaluation}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:eval-intro}

This chapter presents the experimental evaluation of the Layer~3 routing
system described in Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}. The evaluation was
carried out on physical hardware in a three-node testbed that reproduces the
target operational scenario: a mobile robot transmitting a live video stream
to a stationary base station, with an intermediate relay node available to
maintain connectivity after a direct-link failure.

The evaluation pursues two objectives. The first is to characterise the
end-to-end performance of the system: control-channel latency, video
throughput, and recovery time after a link failure. The second, and central
to this dissertation, is to \emph{prove that the nodes respect their TDMA
slots} when transporting both periodic synchronisation beacons and a
continuous video stream, and that this property is preserved when traffic is
forwarded through the relay.

The chapter is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:setup} describes the
hardware, the network and software configuration, the video pipeline, and the
link-failure methodology. Section~\ref{sec:metrics} defines the performance
metrics. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents and analyses each result in a
dedicated subsection. Section~\ref{sec:comparison} compares the system with
the Layer~2 approach of Morais~\cite{morais2024synchronization}.
Section~\ref{sec:eval-summary} summarises the findings.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Testbed Overview}
\label{subsec:overview}

The testbed comprises three nodes, each with a fixed role. The network is an
IEEE~802.11 ad-hoc (IBSS) cell on channel~6. Two addressing planes coexist:
a \emph{physical} plane (\texttt{172.20.10.0/28}) carried by the wireless
interface, and a \emph{mesh} plane (\texttt{10.0.0.0/24}) carried by a TUN
virtual interface, through which all application traffic is routed by the
TDMA framework. The TDMA schedule uses a frame of $T_{\mathrm{frame}} =
150$\,ms divided into three slots of $T_{\mathrm{slot}} = 50$\,ms, one per
node, with a 5\,ms guard interval at the end of each slot. Slot~$i$ is
assigned to node~$i$, giving the fixed ordering
$[\text{N1}, \text{N2}, \text{N3}]$. Figure~\ref{fig:setup} shows the
experimental testbed.

\vspace{0.4cm}
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{setup.png}
\caption{Experimental testbed: the three nodes (N1 robot, N2 relay, N3 base
station) and the TDMA frame of three 50\,ms slots in a 150\,ms period, one per
node.}
\label{fig:setup}
\end{figure}
\vspace{0.5cm}

All three nodes were placed on a laboratory bench within mutual radio range,
so that any pair could communicate directly. This is a deliberate constraint:
because the nodes share a single collision domain, link failures cannot be
produced by distance and must instead be induced in software
(Section~\ref{subsec:linkfail}). The same limitation was reported by
Morais~\cite{morais2024synchronization}.

Beyond the bench, the system was also exercised in a real corridor deployment
to demonstrate live video streaming while the robot is in motion. The base
station (N3) was located in the DaRTES laboratory (Distributed and Real-Time
Embedded Systems, FEUP/DEEC), where this work was developed, and the corridor
outside it was chosen deliberately: its concrete walls attenuate the wireless
signal, so that as the robot drives away from the laboratory the direct
N1\,$\to$\,N3 link is expected to weaken enough to force the traffic through the
relay N2, positioned partway down the corridor. Figure~\ref{fig:setup-mobility}
shows this layout: N3 at the starting point, N2 partway down the corridor, and
the robot N1 driving away. This deployment is used for the qualitative
streaming-in-motion demonstration; the quantitative results reported in this
chapter were taken on the bench setup above, where link failures are induced in
software (Section~\ref{subsec:linkfail}) for repeatability.

Two distinct traffic planes travel over the air, and they must not be confused
when interpreting the captures:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Synchronisation beacons (control).} Each node broadcasts one
  beacon per frame, \emph{within its own slot}, to its neighbours as a small
  UDP packet (physical addresses, ports 7001 to 7003, $\approx 93$ to $107$\,B).
  These carry the adjacency matrix and slot-timing information used by the
  RA-TDMAs+ synchroniser. They are \emph{not} relayed: every node emits its
  own.
  \item \textbf{Application data.} The video stream and the control channel
  travel over the mesh plane (\texttt{10.0.0.x}). The video is an MPEG-TS/UDP
  flow to \texttt{10.0.0.3:5000}; the teleoperation RTT probe is a UDP echo to
  \texttt{10.0.0.1:19876}. When the direct link is unavailable, this traffic
  is forwarded through N2.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Video Pipeline and Application Parameters}
\label{subsec:video-pipeline}

On N1, \texttt{rpicam-vid} captures raw YUV~4:2:0 frames and pipes them to
\texttt{ffmpeg}, which encodes them with \texttt{libx264}, wraps the output in
MPEG-TS, and sends each transport-stream segment as a UDP datagram to
\texttt{10.0.0.3:5000}. Because the destination is a mesh (TUN) address, the
datagram enters the TDMA routing layer transparently. It is worth noting the
two transport layers involved, because they matter for the throughput and
convergence analyses. The video application itself uses UDP, which is the
natural choice for a real-time stream; that UDP traffic is then carried between
the physical nodes by the TDMA framework, whose data plane is built on a TCP
connection over the wireless interface (the framework's periodic beacons, in
contrast, are sent over UDP). The relay therefore forwards the IP datagrams
below this framework transport, a distinction that becomes important in
Sections~\ref{subsec:res-throughput} and~\ref{subsec:res-convergence}. On N3,
\texttt{base\_station.py} receives each datagram, timestamps it, and forwards
it to \texttt{ffplay}. Table~\ref{tab:app-params} lists the parameters.

\subsection{Teleoperation Command Channel}
\label{subsec:cmd-channel}

The video stream described above is only one half of the teleoperation loop; the
other half is the command channel that carries the operator's input from the
base station back to the robot, and it is this channel whose latency ultimately
determines how responsive the robot feels to drive. At N3, \texttt{base\_station.py}
reads a DualShock~4 gamepad through \texttt{pygame} and translates its state into
commands, which it sends as small JSON datagrams over UDP to the robot at
\texttt{10.0.0.1:9000}. Three command types are produced. A \texttt{move}
command carries the per-wheel speeds derived from the triggers and the steering
axis (the right trigger drives forward, the left trigger reverses, and the right
stick steers by biasing the left and right wheel speeds); a \texttt{stop}
command is sent when no movement input is present; and a \texttt{servo} command
carries the pan and tilt angles of the camera mount, adjusted with the
directional pad. Movement commands are emitted at a fixed rate of 20\,Hz (one
every 50\,ms) and servo commands at $\approx 12$\,Hz, so that the channel
produces a steady, low-rate flow of small datagrams rather than bursts. On the
robot, \texttt{alphabot\_node.py} receives each datagram, decodes the JSON, and
actuates the hardware accordingly: the \texttt{move} speeds are written to the
TB6612FNG motor driver and the \texttt{servo} angles to the PCA9685 controller.
A complementary telemetry stream flows in the opposite direction, from the robot
to the base station, as JSON datagrams over UDP port 9001 at 5\,Hz.

Like the video, these command datagrams are addressed to a mesh (\texttt{10.0.0.x})
address, so they are routed by the TDMA framework and are therefore subject to
the same slot structure and, after a direct-link failure, forwarded through N2.
Because the commands are small and slow relative to the video, they never stress
the link capacity; what matters for them is latency and its variability, since a
late or jittery command degrades the feel of the control loop even when no data
is lost. The latency that these commands experience is precisely what the
round-trip measurement of Section~\ref{subsec:res-rtt} characterises, which is
why that measurement uses a UDP echo on the same mesh plane: the RTT probe is a
stand-in for the command that the operator sends and the acknowledgement the
robot would return, and its bounded, slot-quantised distribution is what makes
the teleoperation usable.

\subsection{Link-Failure Methodology}
\label{subsec:linkfail}

Because all nodes share a collision domain, a direct-link failure is induced
logically rather than physically. At the moment of failure, the following
rules are applied on N3:

\begin{verbatim}
iptables -I INPUT  1 -s 172.20.10.1 -j DROP
iptables -I OUTPUT 1 -d 172.20.10.1 -j DROP
\end{verbatim}

These rules discard, at the IP layer, every frame to and from N1's physical
address, making the direct link invisible to N3's TDMA framework and to the
TCP data plane, and forcing traffic onto the relay path
N1\,$\to$\,N2\,$\to$\,N3. The rules are cleared between repetitions with
\texttt{iptables -F}.

To confirm that the block was effective before each relay measurement, N3
attempted to reach N1's physical address with \texttt{ping}
(\texttt{172.20.10.1}); the physical address, unlike the mesh address, is not
reachable through the relay, so a failing ping confirms that the direct link was
genuinely severed and that any subsequent traffic must have travelled through
N2.

\section{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

This section defines the metrics used throughout the evaluation. They are the
standard quantities used to characterise a packet network, adapted where
necessary to the TDMA context in which each leg of a transmission can only
depart within its node's slot.

\subsection{Round-Trip Time (RTT)}
\label{subsec:m-rtt}

The round-trip time is the elapsed time between N3 sending a UDP echo request to
N1 and receiving the corresponding reply. For the $k$-th probe it is given by
Equation~\ref{eq:rtt}, where $t^{\mathrm{tx}}_k$ is the instant the request is
sent and $t^{\mathrm{rx}}_k$ the instant the reply is received, both timestamped
at N3.

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RTT}_k = t^{\mathrm{rx}}_k - t^{\mathrm{tx}}_k
\label{eq:rtt}
\end{equation}

In a TDMA network the RTT reflects the slot structure directly, since each leg
of the round trip can leave its node only during that node's slot.

\subsection{Packet Delivery Ratio (PDR)}
\label{subsec:m-pdr}

The packet delivery ratio is the fraction of echo requests that are answered
within a timeout, expressed as a percentage in Equation~\ref{eq:pdr-m}, where
$N_{\mathrm{rx}}$ is the number of replies received and $N_{\mathrm{tx}}$ the
number of requests sent. It is the complement of the packet loss ratio and
measures the reliability of the path.

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PDR} = \frac{N_{\mathrm{rx}}}{N_{\mathrm{tx}}} \times 100\,\%
\label{eq:pdr-m}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Jitter}
\label{subsec:m-jitter}

Jitter quantifies the variability of the latency, which is critical for
real-time applications such as a live video link. The average relative jitter
used here is the mean absolute difference between consecutive RTT samples,
defined in Equation~\ref{eq:jitter} over $N$ measurements for a given pair of
nodes.

\begin{equation}
J = \frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{k=2}^{N}\bigl\lvert \mathrm{RTT}_k - \mathrm{RTT}_{k-1}\bigr\rvert
\label{eq:jitter}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Throughput}
\label{subsec:m-throughput}

Throughput is the rate at which application data is successfully delivered to
the destination over an observation window. It is computed from the delivered
video payload as in Equation~\ref{eq:throughput}, where $B_{\mathrm{total}}$ is
the sum of the video payload bytes received at N3 during a window of duration
$\Delta t$, and the factor of eight converts bytes to bits.

\begin{equation}
T = \frac{8\,B_{\mathrm{total}}}{\Delta t} \quad [\text{kbps}]
\label{eq:throughput}
\end{equation}

The video application emits an MPEG-TS stream over UDP into the mesh interface;
this traffic is then carried between physical nodes by the TDMA framework, whose
data plane uses a TCP connection (the framework's beacons, by contrast, use
UDP). Throughput is measured at the application level, the delivered video
payload, so that the direct and relay conditions count the same quantity
regardless of how the framework encapsulates it on the wire
(Section~\ref{subsec:res-throughput}).

\subsection{Convergence Time}
\label{subsec:m-conv}

The convergence time is the interval during which the video stream is
interrupted while the system re-routes through the relay after the direct link
fails. It is measured as in Equation~\ref{eq:tconv-m}, the gap between the last
video packet delivered over the direct path and the first packet recovered
through N2.

\begin{equation}
t_{\mathrm{conv}} = t_{\mathrm{first\,relay}} - t_{\mathrm{last\,direct}}
\label{eq:tconv-m}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Inter-Arrival Time (IA)}
\label{subsec:m-ia}

The inter-arrival time is the interval between two consecutive video packets
arriving at N3, defined in Equation~\ref{eq:ia}. Its distribution reveals the
TDMA burst structure of the stream and allows verification that relay forwarding
preserves it.

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IA}_k = t_{k+1} - t_k
\label{eq:ia}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Slot Occupancy}
\label{subsec:m-slot}

To verify that each node transmits only within its assigned slot, the
transmission instant of every captured beacon is wrapped to the frame period, as
in Equation~\ref{eq:phi}, and attributed to its source node.

\begin{equation}
\phi = t \bmod T_{\mathrm{frame}}
\label{eq:phi}
\end{equation}

A node respects its slot if its beacons cluster within its assigned 50\,ms
interval. Because the RA-TDMAs+ synchroniser advances all slots by a common
amount on every round, the whole frame rotates slowly in wall-clock time; this
common rotation is removed before the per-node clustering is evaluated, leaving
the relative synchronisation between the nodes.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

This section is organised so that each subsection isolates one property of the
system: it states briefly how the corresponding test was performed, presents
the figure that supports the result, and analyses it immediately afterwards.

\subsection{Control-Channel Latency}
\label{subsec:res-rtt}

To characterise control-plane latency, N3 sent 100 UDP echo requests to
\texttt{10.0.0.1} at 10\,Hz, with N1 replying to each immediately; the 100
round-trip samples were recorded at N3. The test was performed over the direct
link and over the relay path. Figure~\ref{fig:rtt-direct} shows the result for
the direct link.

\vspace{0.4cm}
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{RTT_over_the_direct_link.png}
\caption{Control-channel RTT over the direct link (N3\,$\to$\,N1, 100 pings,
PDR $= 100\,\%$). Left: RTT per sequence, alternating between a low
($\approx 52$\,ms) and a high ($\approx 202$\,ms) cluster. Right: bimodal
distribution, the clusters one TDMA frame (150\,ms) apart.}
\label{fig:rtt-direct}
\end{figure}
\vspace{0.5cm}

The RTT does not concentrate around a single value; it splits into two
well-separated clusters with an empty band between roughly 60 and 190\,ms.
This is a direct consequence of the slot structure, and it is best understood
by following a single probe through the schedule. The round trip is made of
two independent legs, and each leg can leave its node only during that node's
50\,ms slot. When N3 wishes to send the echo request it cannot transmit
immediately: it must wait until N3's slot comes round, so the request is held
in the transmit queue for up to one frame before it even enters the medium.
Once the request reaches N1, the reply faces the symmetric constraint: it
can depart only inside N1's slot. The total RTT is therefore the sum of the
data-plane transmission time, the processing at each end, and, dominating all
of these, the two quantised waits for the respective slots. Because a wait for
a slot is, by construction, a wait for a position in a periodic 150\,ms frame,
the RTT is built up from whole-frame and fractional-frame components rather
than from a smoothly varying delay.

The two clusters correspond to the two possible outcomes of the second, decisive
slot wait. After the request arrives, the reply either reaches N1's transmit
opportunity in the immediately following frame, producing the lower cluster
at $\approx 52$\,ms, a value fixed by the data-plane encapsulation and the
once-per-frame transmission overhead rather than by any variable queueing, 
or it misses that opportunity by a small margin and must wait one entire frame
for the next one, producing the upper cluster at $\approx 52 + 150 = 202$\,ms.
There is no intermediate outcome: a leg cannot wait ``half a slot extra'',
because the next transmit opportunity is always exactly one frame away. The
cluster separation of precisely one frame (150\,ms), and the empty band between
the clusters, are therefore the signature of TDMA round quantisation: latency
is gained or lost only in whole-frame units. This is the point at which the
present results differ in \emph{shape} from the reference: Morais reports a
broadly triangular RTT histogram, a continuum of values filling the range,
whereas here the histogram is cleanly two-valued. Both shapes arise from the
same slot-waiting mechanism, but the geometry differs. In the reference the RTT
is dominated by the wait between a probe's generation by the free-running ping
and the start of its slot; that wait is spread almost uniformly across the round
and, combined over the two legs, fills the histogram into the triangular shape
Morais describes. Here the within-slot transmit jitter is small, so the residual
variability reduces to a single binary outcome, whether the reply catches its
slot in the same frame or one frame later, collapsing the continuum into two
discrete levels.

The fraction of pings that lands in each cluster is not fixed: it changes from
run to run, and even drifts slowly within a single run. This is not noise but a
deterministic beat. The probe is emitted by a free-running 10\,Hz timer, i.e.\
once every 100\,ms, while the schedule repeats every 150\,ms; because these two
periods are incommensurate, the phase at which successive probes meet N3's slot
walks steadily through the frame, so a probe that catches its slot now will,
several pings later, just miss it, and the samples migrate between the two
clusters in slow blocks, exactly the block structure visible in the
left-hand time series of Figure~\ref{fig:rtt-direct}. A consequence is that the
arithmetic mean and the median of the raw RTT are not meaningful descriptors:
their value depends only on the accidental ratio of fast to slow samples in a
given capture, and a number such as ``a mean of 130\,ms'' describes neither
cluster and would not be reproduced by a second run. For this reason the two
clusters and their separation are reported separately in
Table~\ref{tab:rtt}, rather than a single summary statistic.

The same test over the relay path is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rtt-relay}.

\vspace{0.4cm}
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{RTT_over_the_relay_path.png}
\caption{Control-channel RTT over the relay path (N3\,$\to$\,N2\,$\to$\,N1,
100 pings, PDR $= 100\,\%$). Practically identical to the direct link
(Figure~\ref{fig:rtt-direct}): relaying adds no significant latency.}
\label{fig:rtt-relay}
\end{figure}
\vspace{0.5cm}

The relay distribution is practically identical to the direct one: the same
two clusters, in the same positions, with the same one-frame separation. This
is a stronger statement than it may appear. Inserting N2 into the path could,
in principle, have added a third slot wait, the forwarded packet waiting for
N2's own transmit opportunity, which would have shifted the whole
distribution up by one slot or introduced a third cluster. No such shift is
observed. The explanation is the same as on the data plane: N2 forwards the
control packet through the kernel routing engine within the handling of the
slot in which it arrives, rather than re-queueing it as fresh application
traffic for N2's next slot, so the extra hop costs only a few milliseconds of
processing rather than a full slot of scheduling latency. The control-plane and
data-plane results are thus two views of the same property: the relay is
transparent because forwarding happens below the application and does not
consume a separate scheduling step.

It is worth stating explicitly why this RTT behaviour supports, rather than
merely fails to contradict, the central claim that the nodes respect their
slots. If the nodes were \emph{not} synchronised, if slots overlapped or
drifted unboundedly, the medium would suffer collisions and contention, and
the resulting RTT would be unstructured: a long, ragged tail of values with no
preferred levels, accompanied by packet loss from collided transmissions.
What is observed is the opposite. The latency is confined to two sharp levels
separated by exactly one frame, the maximum stays below two frames, and the
packet delivery ratio is $100\,\%$ in both the direct and relay conditions:
every one of the 100 probes was answered. A network can only produce
quantisation this clean, with no loss, if each node is in fact transmitting
within a well-defined slot and the slots are correctly ordered; the structure
of the RTT is therefore a direct, if indirect-looking, measurement of slot
compliance. Normalising by the frame period, the mean RTT of $\approx 0.9$
frame matches the figure derived from Morais~\cite{morais2024synchronization},
whose reported P2P average of $\approx 88$\,ms over a 100\,ms round corresponds
to 0.88 frame: although the histogram shape differs, the underlying
TDMA-level latency, a round trip costing a little under one frame on average,  is the same, as expected from a shared synchroniser and slot structure.

\subsection{Video Throughput}
\label{subsec:res-throughput}

Throughput is the rate at which video payload is \emph{delivered} to the base
station, and it was measured in a way that is both non-intrusive and fair to
compare between the direct and relay conditions. The video stream produced by
N1 was left running continuously, and a passive capture at N3 observed the
flow for a 15\,s window, recording the size and arrival instant of every video
datagram. The capture is passive in a strict sense: it attaches a raw
\texttt{AF\_PACKET} socket to the interface and reads a copy of each frame,
rather than binding UDP port~5000. This distinction matters. Had the
measurement tool bound port~5000 it would either have collided with the
\texttt{ffplay} receiver (the operating system does not allow two ordinary
sockets to own the same port) or, worse, silently stolen datagrams from the
player and distorted the very quantity being measured. By reading a copy below
the socket layer, the tool coexists with the live player and observes exactly
the bytes the application receives, without perturbing the stream. The
experiment was repeated three times over the direct link and three times over
the relay path, and the results were averaged to suppress the run-to-run
variation that the variable-bitrate encoder introduces as the scene changes.

The quantity reported here is the \emph{application-level} throughput: the
MPEG-TS video payload delivered end-to-end from N1 to N3, identified by the
mesh flow \texttt{10.0.0.1}\,$\to$\,\texttt{10.0.0.3:5000}. It deliberately
excludes the wire-level framing, and this choice is what makes the direct and
relay numbers comparable. On the direct path the video is carried inside the
TDMA framework's own transport encapsulation, whereas on the relay path N2
forwards the raw IP datagrams; counting the encapsulated wire bytes would
therefore measure two different things and would wrongly suggest that the relay
halves the throughput. Counting the delivered video payload instead measures
the same thing in both cases, the bytes the player actually consumes, and
isolates the effect of the relay hop from the framing overhead. A useful
by-product of inspecting the relay capture is direct evidence that the
forwarding is transparent: the relayed video appears on the wire as plain
MPEG-TS/UDP datagrams addressed \texttt{10.0.0.1}\,$\to$\,\texttt{10.0.0.3},
\emph{not} re-wrapped in any additional framework header. The relay is thus a
genuine network-layer forward (the kernel routing engine moving an IP packet
from one interface to the other), not a re-origination of the stream.
Figure~\ref{fig:throughput} and Table~\ref{tab:throughput} report the means.

The direct-link mean of 558\,kbps sits about 12\,\% above the 500\,kbps figure
configured in the encoder. This is expected, and decomposing it is instructive
because the gap is structural rather than a measurement artefact. The 500\,kbps
target governs the \emph{compressed video elementary stream} that
\texttt{libx264} emits; it does not include the cost of packaging that stream
for transport. Two mechanisms add the remaining bytes.

First, the MPEG-TS container imposes a fixed framing tax. The elementary stream
is segmented into 188-byte transport packets, each carrying a 4-byte header, so
the container alone inflates the payload by $4/184 \approx 2.2\,\%$ before it
ever reaches the network; the UDP datagrams then group seven such transport
packets ($7 \times 188 = 1316$\,B) and, once the UDP (8\,B) and IP (20\,B)
headers are added, the on-the-wire framing accounts for a further few per cent.
Together the containerisation and headers explain the bulk of the excess.

Second, and more significantly, the encoder is configured with \texttt{-g~1},
which forces every frame to be coded as an independent intra (I) frame and
disables inter-frame prediction entirely. This choice is deliberate: with an
all-intra stream the loss of any single packet damages only one frame and the
video self-heals on the next frame, which is the behaviour wanted for a
low-latency teleoperation link over a lossy wireless medium. The cost is rate:
without P- or B-frames the encoder cannot exploit temporal redundancy between
successive frames, so each frame is encoded from scratch and the rate
controller produces a higher and far more constant bitrate than an equivalent
GOP-structured stream would. The 558\,kbps observed is therefore the natural
operating point of a robustness-oriented, all-intra configuration, not a
malfunction of the rate control.

A 558\,kbps stream is comfortably within the capacity that the schedule grants
to N1, which is the reason the stream is sustained without loss despite being
confined to one slot in three. N1 may transmit only during its 50\,ms slot of
every 150\,ms frame, i.e.\ for one third of wall-clock time. To deliver a
mean of 558\,kbps while transmitting for only a third of the time, the
\emph{instantaneous} rate inside the slot must be roughly three times the mean,
about 1.7\,Mbps. This is a small fraction of the raw rate available on the
IEEE~802.11 channel, so the slot has ample headroom: the burst of $\approx 8$
datagrams that constitutes one frame's worth of video (derived in
Equation~\ref{eq:pkts-per-slot}, Section~\ref{subsec:res-ia}) drains well
within the 50\,ms slot, leaving the guard interval clear. This headroom is what
allows the stream to coexist with the periodic beacons and the teleoperation
control traffic inside the same slot structure without any of them being
starved, and it is the quantitative reason the schedule is never the limiting
factor on the video plane.

Under relay conditions the mean throughput is 552\,kbps, a reduction of only
$\approx 1\,\%$ relative to the direct path. This difference is smaller than
the run-to-run variation the variable-bitrate encoder produces as the scene
content changes, so it cannot be attributed to the relay hop; no systematic
degradation is observed. The result is significant because, a priori, inserting
an intermediate node could have cost a full extra slot of latency per frame and
a corresponding reduction in sustained rate if N2 had to schedule the forwarded
video into its \emph{own} transmit opportunity. It does not. As established
above, N2 forwards the raw IP datagrams through the kernel routing engine
rather than re-originating them as fresh application traffic, so the video is
carried within the existing slot structure instead of consuming a separate
TDMA scheduling step at the relay. The relay is therefore effectively
\emph{transparent} on the video plane, and this is the data-plane counterpart
of the control-plane result of Section~\ref{subsec:res-rtt}, where forwarding
through N2 likewise added no measurable latency. The two observations reinforce
each other: the same forwarding mechanism that leaves the round-trip
distribution unchanged also leaves the delivered throughput unchanged.

At first sight this $\approx 1\,\%$ result appears to contradict the reference
work of Morais~\cite{morais2024synchronization}, which reported a severe
throughput reduction with relaying, to roughly one half of the single-hop
value with one relay and, with a second relay, to about one fifth of that
one-relay value again, an order of magnitude below the single-hop rate. The
apparent contradiction dissolves once the two measurements are recognised as
probing different regimes, and the contrast is itself informative.

The reduction reported in the reference is the classic capacity collapse of a
single-channel multi-hop wireless chain. When every node shares one radio
channel and the medium is half-duplex, a packet forwarded by a relay must be
\emph{received} and then \emph{re-transmitted} on the same channel; the relay's
two transmissions, plus those of the source, contend for one shared resource,
so the maximum end-to-end rate falls roughly as $C/n$ with the number of hops
$n$. Crucially, that ceiling is only \emph{reached}, and therefore only
\emph{measured}, by a flow that deliberately saturates the link. The reference
obtained its figures with \texttt{iperf3}, whose explicit purpose is to push as
much TCP traffic as the medium will accept until congestion and retransmissions
cap the rate; under that load, and under CSMA/CA with no slotting, the per-hop
halving is exactly what should appear.

The measurement here is of a fundamentally different kind. The video is a
\emph{fixed-rate, non-saturating} application stream of $\approx 558$\,kbps,
which is between one and two per cent of the per-hop capacity, even after a
single relay hop has halved that capacity, several megabits per second of
headroom remain. The quantity of interest is therefore not the link's maximum
capacity but whether the offered rate is \emph{preserved} end to end, and at a
load this far below the ceiling the second hop forwards the stream without ever
throttling it. The bottleneck that produces the collapse binds only as the
offered load approaches capacity; our application never operates in that
regime. Two further factors widen the gap: the TDMA schedule eliminates the
CSMA/CA collisions and retransmissions that account for much of the reference's
loss, and, as established above, the kernel forward at N2 does not consume a
separate TDMA slot. We did not run a saturating \texttt{iperf3} test on the relay
path, because it does not correspond to how the system is used; in such a test
the per-hop capacity sharing of the shared medium would naturally reappear, but
that is a property of single-channel multi-hop wireless rather than of the
forwarding design. For the application this system is built to carry, the
operationally relevant regime is the source-limited one, and in that regime the
relay is transparent.

The 558/552\,kbps figures are time-averages, but the instantaneous delivery is
far from flat: because N1 may transmit only inside its slot, the video arrives
in bursts separated by silent inter-burst gaps of one frame, and the mean
throughput is the integral of this bursty profile over the observation window.
This temporal structure is exactly what the inter-arrival analysis of
Section~\ref{subsec:res-ia} quantifies, roughly 88\,\% of the datagrams
arrive back-to-back within a burst and the remainder are separated by a
one-frame gap, and it is identical for the direct and relay conditions. The
flatness of the \emph{averaged} throughput over the window therefore does not
contradict the burstiness of the stream; it is the expected consequence of a
constant-rate encoder feeding a periodic slot, sampled over many frames.

\subsection{Routing Convergence}
\label{subsec:res-convergence}

Convergence is the time the system takes to restore the video stream through
the relay after the direct link fails. It was measured entirely at N3 from the
passive video capture, as follows. First, the direct-link stream was allowed
to stabilise for at least three seconds. The \texttt{iptables} rules of
Section~\ref{subsec:linkfail} were then applied to sever the direct link. The
capture was monitored for two events: the \emph{gap}, defined as the instant
no video packet had arrived for more than $0.4$\,s (its timestamp,
$t_{\mathrm{last\,direct}}$, marks the last packet delivered over the direct
path); and the \emph{recovery}, defined as the arrival of the first video
packet after the gap, forwarded through N2 ($t_{\mathrm{first\,relay}}$). The
convergence time is
\begin{equation}
t_{\mathrm{conv}} = t_{\mathrm{first\,relay}} - t_{\mathrm{last\,direct}}.
\label{eq:tconv}
\end{equation}
The direct link was then restored and the rules cleared before the next
repetition. Three repetitions were performed; Table~\ref{tab:convergence}
reports the results.

The three values (2675, 2768, and 2863\,ms) are close, with a median of
$\approx 2.8$\,s, equivalent to about 18~TDMA frames. Their tight spread is
itself informative: a recovery governed by a variable process such as contention
or retransmission would scatter far more widely, whereas one governed by a fixed
timeout clusters tightly, exactly as observed. This already points to the
dominant cost being a single constant rather than the routing work, which the
breakdown below confirms.

The dominant component of the convergence time is the \emph{link-failure
detection}, which is governed by the node-aging mechanism of the RA-TDMAs+
adjacency matrix. Each node keeps, for every neighbour, the timestamp of the
last beacon received from it; the neighbour's \emph{age} is the elapsed time
since that beacon. On every round the function \texttt{removeDeadLinks()}
compares each age against the constant \texttt{MAX\_AGE}, and a neighbour whose
age reaches \texttt{MAX\_AGE} is declared dead and removed from the matrix. In
this implementation \texttt{MAX\_AGE} $= 2.0$\,s, so when the direct link to N1
is severed, N3 stops receiving N1's beacons but continues to treat N1 as a live
neighbour for a full $2.0$\,s before the link is removed. This is a deliberate
design feature, not an accident of timing: because the three nodes share one
collision domain, an individual beacon is occasionally lost to a transient
collision even when the link is perfectly healthy, and declaring a neighbour
dead on the first missed beacon would cause the topology to flap continuously.
The aging timeout is the hysteresis that prevents this, it requires the
absence of beacons to persist for $2.0$\,s, i.e.\ for roughly thirteen
consecutive missed beacons at the 150\,ms frame, before believing the link is
truly gone. Only once N1 has aged out does the topology change and the route via
N2 become installable. The recovery chain therefore comprises the three stages
of Table~\ref{tab:conv-breakdown}.

The breakdown shows that the route computation itself is negligible
(microseconds on a three-node graph): the convergence time is set almost
entirely by the $2.0$\,s aging timeout, plus a sub-second tail for the relay
path to be established and the first forwarded packet to arrive. This
localisation of the cost has a clear practical consequence. Because detection
dominates and route installation is essentially free, the convergence time is
not a property of the routing layer's efficiency at all, a faster routing
algorithm or a smaller graph would not change it, but a property of a single
tunable constant. The most direct way to accelerate convergence is therefore to
reduce \texttt{MAX\_AGE}: halving it to $1.0$\,s would roughly halve the
recovery time, and the lower bound on detection is set only by how aggressively
one is willing to age out links. That aggressiveness is precisely the trade-off.
A shorter timeout recovers faster from genuine failures but is more likely to
mistake a transient burst of collisions for a dead link, tearing down and
rebuilding a healthy route and momentarily interrupting the very stream it is
meant to protect; a longer timeout is more robust to sporadic beacon loss but
slower to react. The chosen value of $2.0$\,s sits deliberately on the
conservative side of this trade-off, favouring stability of the topology in the
shared collision domain over raw recovery speed, which is the appropriate
choice for a teleoperation link where a spurious route flap is more disruptive
than a bounded, one-off recovery delay. Tuning this constant, or replacing the
fixed timeout with an adaptive scheme that reacts to the recent beacon-loss
rate, is identified as a concrete avenue for future work.

It is worth being precise about the origin of the sub-second tail, and about how
large it should be expected to be, because it reflects a genuine architectural
difference from the Layer~2 reference rather than an inefficiency. The tail is
not route computation, which is microseconds. It is also not, as one might first
assume, the cost of opening a connection: the TCP links between neighbours are
held open continuously by the keepalive thread, so when the route changes the
source performs no handshake, it simply begins sending over a socket it already
holds. The real difference is that our relay path is \emph{connection-oriented
and rises through the protocol stack}, whereas the Layer~2 reference forwards by
a stateless MAC rewrite that never leaves the kernel. In our design the first
hop N1\,$\to$\,N2 travels over TCP and is delivered up to the user-space receive
thread at N2 before being handed back to the kernel
(\texttt{tun\_write}\,$\to$\,\texttt{ip\_forward}) for the second hop to N3. Per
packet this extra trip up the stack is sub-millisecond, far too small to see at
the TDMA timescale. A delay relative to the
connectionless Layer~2 method is therefore expected, but on this evidence a
\emph{small} one: the per-packet overhead alone cannot account for a tail of
hundreds of milliseconds. What remains of the sub-second tail is best attributed
not to that per-packet hop but to the connection-oriented data plane re-engaging
after the break, the stream that had been flowing over the now-dead direct
connection must be abandoned and taken up by the relay path before video reaches
N3 again, a transition a stateless forwarder never has to make. The practical
impact is a modest sub-second penalty added to detection on each recovery, and a
negligible per-packet user-space cost in steady state.

This narrow step is where the Layer~2 approach of Morais would be faster, and it
is worth being precise about \emph{which} step, because the rest is comparable.
Detection is identical: that design removes a dead link through the same
connectivity-matrix aging, so it incurs the same \texttt{MAX\_AGE} wait before
anything can change (Morais does not report a convergence figure; this follows
from both designs sharing the RA-TDMAs+ adjacency matrix and its aging
mechanism). Route recomputation and installation are close but not free of
asymmetry, and here the cost is ours: the reference recomputes its linked-list
routes from the spanning tree and writes a single ARP entry per destination with
one \texttt{ioctl()}, whereas we recompute from the same spanning tree but
install \emph{two} routes per destination over Netlink, one in the main table
and one in the relay table~200 (Section~\ref{sec:kernel-config}). Two Netlink
messages across two tables are heavier than a single ARP rewrite, a cost we
accept for the dual-table separation; both nonetheless complete in well under a
slot, so neither is where the gap lies. The same asymmetry, and the same verdict,
applies to the per-packet forwarding itself: the reference relay performs one
link-layer MAC rewrite that never leaves the kernel, whereas ours carries each
forwarded packet up to the user-space TCP receive thread at N2 and back down
through \texttt{tun\_write} and \texttt{ip\_forward}. We accept that this is
measurably more processing per forwarded packet; it is simply too small,
sub-millisecond, to register against the 50\,ms slot, which is why the relay
added no measurable latency in steady state (Section~\ref{subsec:res-rtt}). The
difference that does matter is at the source at the moment of recovery. The
Layer~2 source is connectionless: once the ARP entry points at the new next
hop, the very next frame is relayed by the kernel at the link layer, with
nothing to wind down. Ours is connection-oriented: before the stream can resume
through N2, N1 must abandon the TCP connection to the now-unreachable N3, on
which its transmitter can stall up to the one-second send timeout
(\texttt{SO\_SNDTIMEO}, Section~\ref{subsec:tcp-socket-config}) while the
buffered frames time out. The connection it switches to, N1\,$\to$\,N2, is
already open, so no handshake is paid, and the relay forwarding at N2 is a
kernel \texttt{ip\_forward} that costs nothing to bring up. It is therefore this
source-side wind-down alone (not the detection, nor the route computation, nor
the route install, nor the relay forwarding, all of which are matched or
cheaper) that the Layer~2 design avoids by construction, shrinking the
$\approx 0.8$\,s tail toward a single round.

The tail is thus the price paid for operating at Layer~3 over a TCP data plane,
the same choice that buys reliable in-order delivery, standard observability, and
kernel integration (Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}). It is a deliberate trade,
not an oversight: detection ($2.0$\,s) dominates the total today, so the penalty
is secondary; but if \texttt{MAX\_AGE} were reduced, this data-plane transfer cost
would become the next bottleneck, and a lighter-weight, connectionless forwarding
path closer to the Layer~2 design would then be the lever to pull.

\subsection{TDMA Slot Synchronisation}
\label{subsec:res-slots}

The previous subsections show that the end-to-end timing is consistent with
the schedule. This subsection proves the schedule directly, by examining the
instant at which each node transmits. The video stream is generated only by
N1, and therefore evidences only N1's slot; the synchronisation beacons,
however, are emitted by all three nodes and reveal the complete slot
structure. The beacons captured at N3 were wrapped to the 150\,ms frame and
attributed to their source node; Figure~\ref{fig:slots} shows the result over
a sequence of rounds.

\vspace{0.4cm}
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{ao_rounds_derot.png}
\caption{Beacon instants wrapped to the frame, per round, after removing the
common frame rotation. Each node keeps its own slot in cyclic order
N1\,$\to$\,N2\,$\to$\,N3, $\approx 50$\,ms apart: direct evidence of slot
compliance.}
\label{fig:slots}
\end{figure}
\vspace{0.5cm}

The data underlying Figure~\ref{fig:slots} were obtained by capturing the
beacon traffic at N3 with a passive sniffer and recording, for each beacon, its
arrival instant and its source node (identifiable from the physical address and
the source port, 7001 to 7003). The capture taps the wireless interface directly,
below the firewall, which is important for two reasons. First, it sees every
beacon that reaches the antenna regardless of any \texttt{iptables} rule, so the
same method works unchanged in the direct and relay conditions. Second, it
observes the transmissions as they actually occur on the medium, which is what a
claim about slot occupancy must be about.

This differs from the reference work, which dedicated a fourth, passive monitor
node to observing the medium; here the measurement is taken from N3, an
\emph{active participant}. That choice carries a minor, known cost: the radio is
half-duplex, so N3 cannot capture during its own transmission, and its own slot
is therefore seen from the transmit side while the other two are seen on
reception, a small per-node timing asymmetry; N3's own transmissions also occupy
part of the medium it is measuring. We accept this because it needs no additional
hardware or observer node; because N3 is in radio range of both N1 and N2 and so
receives all three beacons, giving the complete slot structure; and because it is
precisely the base station's operational viewpoint, which is the perspective that
matters for a teleoperation link. The asymmetry amounts to a few milliseconds of
receive-path delay, far below the 50\,ms slot, so it nudges the absolute positions
slightly but leaves the slot ordering and the inter-node spacing (the
quantities the claim depends on) intact.

The three nodes occupy three disjoint regions in the cyclic order
N1\,$\to$\,N2\,$\to$\,N3, each separated from the next by approximately one
slot, and each remains in its region across rounds. Quantitatively, after the
processing described below, the per-node standard deviation of the transmission
instant is $\approx 16$\,ms, small relative to the 50\,ms slot, and
84 to 89\,\% of all beacons fall within their node's assigned slot, the remainder
lying in the 5\,ms guard band or spilling marginally into a neighbour's slot
under transient load. That the three clouds neither overlap nor exchange order
across the captured rounds is the direct, transmitter-side evidence that the
schedule is being respected: each node is in fact confining its transmissions
to its own slot, which is the property the whole evaluation set out to
establish.

Two characteristics of the RA-TDMAs+ synchroniser must be understood to read
the figure correctly, and both stem from the fact that the network has no
global clock. First, the absolute phase of the frame is arbitrary: there is no
external reference that fixes where ``time zero'' of the frame lies, so only the
\emph{relative} ordering and spacing of the nodes carry physical meaning. A plot
in which all three clouds were shifted bodily by the same amount would describe
an equally valid, equally synchronised network; it is the gaps between the
nodes, not their absolute positions, that matter. Second, the synchroniser does
not hold the frame fixed in wall-clock time. To keep the nodes aligned it
advances all slots by a small common amount on every round, with the effect that
the entire frame rotates slowly against wall-clock time, at $\approx 18$\,ms/s
in these captures. This rotation is visible in Figure~\ref{fig:slots-drift},
which plots the same beacons \emph{without} the de-rotation applied: the three
clouds sweep diagonally across the frame as the rounds advance, each node moving
by the same amount per round so that their mutual spacing is preserved while
their absolute positions drift. The rotation is therefore a property of all
nodes together and says nothing about their mutual alignment, so it is removed
(the per-round common offset is subtracted) before the clustering is evaluated,
which yields the stationary view of Figure~\ref{fig:slots}; what remains after
de-rotation is the relative synchronisation between nodes, the quantity of
interest. Over a window of only a few rounds the rotation is small and could be
neglected, but over a longer capture it must be de-rotated explicitly or the
clouds would smear across the frame and obscure the very alignment they are
meant to show.

\caption{The same beacons as Figure~\ref{fig:slots}, without removing the
frame rotation: the three clouds drift together at $\approx 18$\,ms/s, keeping

\label{fig:slots-drift}
\end{figure}
\vspace{0.5cm}

Under relay conditions the same analysis confirms that the cyclic ordering is
maintained. This case requires care, because a naive reading of the capture
seems paradoxical: with the direct link to N1 blocked, how can N1's beacons
still appear at N3 and still fall in N1's slot? The resolution lies in where the
two operations act. The \texttt{iptables} rule discards N1's packets at the IP
layer, whereas the passive sniffer taps the wireless interface below it, before
the firewall. N1's beacons are therefore removed from N3's TDMA
\emph{application}, which is why N3 stops treating N1 as a direct neighbour
and falls back to synchronising through the two-hop chain
N1\,$\to$\,N2\,$\to$\,N3, while still being \emph{recorded} by the capture,
because they continue to reach the antenna. The capture thus shows
the true state of the medium (N1 is still transmitting, still in its slot),
whereas the routing layer reflects the logical topology (N1 is unreachable
directly). Far from undermining the result, this confirms two things at once:
that N1 keeps its slot regardless of how N3 routes to it, and that the
link-failure abstraction is being imposed at the right layer. The only
observable effect of the relay condition is a slightly looser alignment of N3
relative to N1, which is expected, since N3 now derives its timing indirectly
through N2 rather than directly from N1 and therefore inherits one extra hop of
synchronisation slack.

\subsubsection{Direct Evidence: the Video Burst Inside N1's Slot}
\label{subsubsec:video-slot}

The beacon analysis above proves the slot structure, but the beacons are a
low-rate control signal: one short packet per node per frame. A stronger test
is whether the \emph{high-rate video stream} also stays within a slot. Because
the video is sourced only by N1, it probes N1's slot specifically, and it can
be placed in the frame with the same method used for the beacons.

A single unfiltered capture was taken at N3 on the wireless interface while N1
streamed video over the direct link, so that the beacons and the video appear in
the same trace and therefore in the same time base. Two packet classes were then
separated. The MATRIX \emph{beacons} were identified by the TDMA control
protocol and their small size ($\le 120$\,B) and tagged with their source node.
N1's \emph{video} packets were identified by N1's source address and their large
size ($\ge 200$\,B); the size threshold is essential, because it excludes the
small ($\approx 66$\,B) transport-level acknowledgements that the data plane
exchanges in the opposite direction and that would otherwise be mistaken for
control traffic. Both classes were wrapped to the 150\,ms frame
(Equation~\ref{eq:phi}). The common frame rotation was estimated once, on the
beacons, as the rotation rate that maximises the per-node angular concentration
(a circular estimator robust to the several packets each node emits per frame
and to the slow, slightly non-linear drift of a long capture), and the
\emph{same} rotation was removed from both classes, so that the beacon and the
video live in one consistent de-rotated frame. The analysis is restricted to a
short window of rounds, over which the rotation is essentially linear.
Figure~\ref{fig:video-slot} overlays the result.

\vspace{0.4cm}
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{stream_round.png}
\caption{Video and beacon instants wrapped to the frame, per round
(de-rotated). N1's video burst (black~$\times$) sits in N1's slot, immediately
after N1's beacon; under peak load and residual de-rotation drift its tail

N3 slot. The video, not just the beacons, tracks the schedule.}
\label{fig:video-slot}
\end{figure}
\vspace{0.4cm}

The video packets form a dense per-round burst that begins immediately after
N1's beacon and occupies most of N1's slot, well separated from the N3 slot at
the far end of the frame. The burst is not, however, perfectly contained: at
several rounds its tail crosses the 50\,ms boundary and reaches into the start
of N2's slot, and these incursions are plainly visible in
Figure~\ref{fig:video-slot}. We address them directly rather than gloss over
them, because understanding their cause is what turns the figure into evidence
rather than a worry. Two effects combine to produce the spillover, and neither
amounts to a loss of slot discipline.

The first, and the more fundamental, lies in the data plane. When the
transmitter calls \texttt{send()} during N1's slot it does not put the bytes on
the air: it hands them to the kernel's TCP/IP stack, which then schedules their
transmission on its own terms, TCP flow and congestion control followed by
CSMA/CA medium access. The framework therefore controls \emph{how much} it
offers per slot but not the exact instant each byte leaves the antenna, so a
batch of packets handed over near the slot end can be put on the air by the
kernel a few milliseconds into the next slot. This is exactly where the per-slot
byte budget and back-pressure mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:slot-control}) acts,
and it is the \emph{only} lever available: at \texttt{slot\_end} the transmitter
stops dequeuing and returns the next packet to its queue, bounding the volume of
data offered to the kernel within the slot. It works as a coarse bound, the
burst stays localised to N1's slot round after round rather than smearing across
the frame, but it cannot enforce a hard wire-level boundary, because once the
data is below the socket the transmit timing belongs to the kernel and is no
longer ours to schedule. The visible incursions are the signature of that limit,
not of the transmitter losing its slot, and tightening the boundary, by gating
transmission below the TCP layer or pacing it within the slot, is a concrete
improvement we identify as future work (Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusions}). The
second effect is a measurement artefact of the de-rotation.
The common frame rotation was estimated on the beacons, and the video's residual
phase is not removed perfectly over the window, so the plotted burst wanders by
a few milliseconds from round to round, which exaggerates the apparent incursion
relative to the true airtime. The slow drift of the burst's position across
rounds in Figure~\ref{fig:video-slot} is the visible signature of this residual
rotation, not of the transmitter losing its slot.

The incursions are thus small, occasional, and bounded, consistent with the
84 to 89\,\% in-slot fraction measured for the beacons, and they overlap only
the guard region and the very start of N2's slot, where any contention is
resolved by the underlying CSMA/CA rather than causing a hard collision. The
essential observation stands: the burst tracks N1's slot round after round
instead of spreading across the frame, which is the behaviour an out-of-slot or
desynchronised transmitter could not produce.

One further detail belongs to the same boundary. The slot discipline applies to
the application \emph{payload} that the transmitter thread sends, not to every
byte the kernel puts on the air: because the data plane runs over TCP, the
kernel emits bare acknowledgements (a few tens of bytes) outside N1's slot,
since they are generated by the kernel stack rather than by the slot-gated
transmitter. These are negligible in size, carry no video, and are absorbed by
CSMA/CA; they simply mark the point where the framework's slot control ends and
the kernel's autonomous traffic begins, the same boundary already noted at the
relay (Section~\ref{subsec:res-throughput}).

Combined with the construction argument (in the direct configuration only N1
sources the video, and the TDMA MAC permits it to transmit solely within its own
slot), this closes the gap between the inter-arrival evidence
(Section~\ref{subsec:res-ia}), which shows the video is bursty with a one-frame
period, and the slot structure: the video burst is not merely periodic, it sits
in N1's slot.

Together, three independent measurements establish the central claim of this
evaluation: the inter-arrival analysis (Section~\ref{subsec:res-ia}) shows the
video is periodic with a one-frame period; this overlay places that burst inside
N1's slot; and the beacon analysis fixes all three slots. The nodes therefore
respect their TDMA slots, for both periodic control traffic and continuous video,
and this property is preserved under relaying.

\section{Comparison with the Layer~2 Approach}
\label{sec:comparison}

The system evaluated here implements routing at Layer~3 (IP), using kernel
forwarding and standard routing tables over a TUN overlay. The prior work of
Morais~\cite{morais2024synchronization} implements routing at Layer~2, by
manipulating ARP tables so that the relay forwards frames transparently below
IP. It is worth being concrete about what this means for the relay hop, because
the two designs differ exactly there. In the Layer~2 method the relayed packet
still transits the intermediate node, N2 is not bypassed, but N2 forwards it by
simply rewriting the frame's destination MAC to the next hop and retransmitting
it at the link layer, leaving the IP header untouched and never lifting the
packet into user space. Our relay does strictly more: it carries each forwarded
packet up to the user-space TCP receive thread at N2 and then back down through
\texttt{tun\_write} and the kernel's IP-forwarding engine. Both run on the same
RA-TDMAs+ synchroniser and the same class of hardware. To compare the two on equal terms, on a single testbed and under the
same conditions, rather than across separate experiments, the Layer~2 method
was re-implemented and run on the same three-node testbed used throughout this
chapter. Section~\ref{subsec:l2-rerun} first describes that re-implementation
and the measurements it produced; Section~\ref{subsec:l2-comparison} then draws
the side-by-side comparison, with latency figures normalised to the frame
period to account for the different frame durations (100\,ms with four nodes in
the reference, 150\,ms with three nodes here).

\subsection{Re-implementation of the Layer~2 Method}
\label{subsec:l2-rerun}

The figures reported by Morais were obtained on a different testbed at a
different time. To place the comparison that follows on a firmer footing, the
Layer~2 method was re-implemented and run on the same three-node testbed used
throughout this chapter, under the same conditions, so that the two approaches
could be measured directly against each other rather than across separate
experiments. It must be stated plainly that the source code of the reference
implementation was not available to us, so this Layer~2 system was reconstructed
entirely from the published description in the dissertation of
Morais~\cite{morais2024synchronization}. It is therefore a best-effort
re-creation rather than the original software, and it may diverge from it in
details that the report does not specify; any shortcoming observed here could in
part reflect our reconstruction rather than the method itself. That same lack of
access is precisely what made a re-implementation necessary: the stated goal was
to compare the two approaches on equal terms, on a single testbed, and with no
access to the original code the only way to do so was to rebuild the Layer~2
method ourselves from its description. The re-implementation follows the
reference faithfully, in the terms its report sets out: forwarding is performed
at the data link layer by installing static ARP entries that map each
destination's address to the next-hop MAC through \texttt{ioctl(SIOCSARP)}; the
relay then retransmits each frame toward that next-hop MAC at the link layer,
leaving the IP header and the end-to-end source and destination addresses
untouched, rather than lifting the packet into the kernel's IP-forwarding path
as our own Layer~3 design does; the video is carried over UDP; and the routing
module builds the same binary spanning tree from the connectivity matrix. The decisive point for a fair comparison is that both
methods run on the identical RA-TDMAs+ synchroniser and the identical
TDMA-agnostic framework, and differ only in the routing and forwarding layer, so
any difference that appears is attributable to that layer alone and not to the
platform, the schedule, or the measurement tooling. The encoder was kept at the
same 500\,kbps configuration used for the Layer~3 results, and the node-aging
timeout at the same \texttt{MAX\_AGE} $= 2.0$\,s, to keep the comparison equal
for equal.

\textbf{Convergence.} The convergence time, measured exactly as in
Section~\ref{subsec:res-convergence} (the gap in the delivered video between the
last packet over the direct path and the first packet forwarded through N2), is
reported in Table~\ref{tab:l2-conv}. The re-implemented Layer~2 method recovers
in a median of $\approx 2.25$\,s, against the $\approx 2.8$\,s of the Layer~3
system, a difference of roughly half a second, and with a far tighter spread:
the three Layer~2 runs fall within 4\,ms of one another, against a spread of
nearly 200\,ms for Layer~3. This is precisely the outcome anticipated in
Section~\ref{subsec:res-convergence}. Both methods pay the same $2.0$\,s
detection cost, because both detect the dead link through the same node-aging
timeout; what separates them is what happens at the source after detection.
The Layer~2 path is connectionless, so once the ARP entry points at N2 the very
next frame is relayed by the kernel and the stream resumes almost immediately,
leaving only a short tail of about a quarter of a second. On the Layer~3 side
the relay forwarding is just as cheap, a kernel \texttt{ip\_forward} of the same
order, but the source first has to wind down the TCP connection to the
now-unreachable N3, on which its transmitter can stall up to the one-second send
timeout before the stream settles onto the already-open connection to N2; that
source-side connection state is the $\approx 0.8$\,s tail isolated earlier. The
measured gap of $\approx 0.5$\,s between the two totals is therefore the
empirical price of the connection-oriented data plane at the source, and it
confirms, on the same hardware, the architectural
analysis that had previously been argued only from the reference's reported
numbers.

\textbf{Streaming stability.} The convergence figure tells only half of the
story, because it marks when the first relayed packet arrives, not whether the
stream that follows is usable. Here the re-implementation exposed a genuine
difference. After recovery, the relayed video did not settle into a smooth flow
on our testbed: it arrived in bursts roughly one node-aging period apart, with
the topology repeatedly losing and regaining its direct neighbours, so that the
picture froze and jumped rather than playing continuously. Reducing the encoder
bitrate did not remove the effect, and that is the key observation, because it
shows the cause is not a shortage of raw bandwidth.

The mechanism is the interaction between the Layer~2 relay and the shared
collision domain. The relay forwards at the link layer and is not slot-aware:
the instant N2 receives a video frame from N1 it re-transmits it, which happens
inside N1's slot rather than within a slot of N2's own. With the
three nodes in mutual range on one channel, these out-of-slot relay
transmissions contend with, and collide with, the synchronisation beacons, and
the resulting beacon loss stresses the RA-TDMAs+ synchroniser. This coupling is
not a surprise: Morais herself documents it, noting that forwarding delays at
the kernel level can place stress on the network and end up ``influencing the
synchronization protocol''. In our conditions the coupling was strong enough to
push neighbours past the $2.0$\,s aging timeout in bursts, so the route
oscillated between direct and relayed and the video was delivered in the
periodic bursts described above. The effect is therefore a property of the
shared medium and the non-slotted relay, not of the offered bitrate, which is
why lowering the rate did not cure it.

Two factors specific to our setup make this more severe than in the reference.
First, the failure is induced with \texttt{iptables} rather than by distance, so
N1 keeps transmitting on the channel even after it has been logically removed,
and its transmissions continue to contend for the medium on which N3 is trying
to receive N2; a genuine out-of-range failure would have silenced N1 and freed
the channel. Second, the testbed places all three nodes within mutual range, the
very condition Morais identifies as worst for collisions when she observes that
an ideal testbed would contain nodes that cannot hear one another, and that at
multi-hop ``the entire network becomes congested''. The Layer~3 system did not
show the same instability under identical conditions, which we attribute to its
TCP data plane recovering the packets lost to these collisions and to its
slot-gated source, though part of the difference may also reflect the particular
run conditions. We report the instability plainly, as an observed limitation of
the re-implementation on this testbed rather than a fundamental verdict on the
Layer~2 method, which the reference shows to function. Because our Layer~2 system
was reconstructed from the published description and not from the original
source, part of the instability may equally stem from a detail of the original
implementation that the report does not document and that our re-creation does
not reproduce. Separating how much is intrinsic to the design, how much is an
artefact of the shared-domain testbed and the software-induced failure, and how
much is a limitation of our reconstruction from the report, is left as future
work.

At the TDMA level the two approaches behave essentially the same way: the
RTT, PDR and slot-compliance rows of the table line up, which is expected since
both run on the same synchroniser and the same slot structure. What is worth
discussing, then, is not where they agree but where they differ, and those
differences are architectural rather than numerical.

\textbf{Latency distribution.} The reference reports a spread, triangular RTT
distribution, whereas this work observes a clean bimodal distribution. Both
are manifestations of the same slot-waiting mechanism; the bimodal shape
arises here because, with three nodes and the requester/responder slots
adjacent across the frame boundary, the reply either catches the next slot or
misses it by exactly one frame, producing two discrete levels rather than a
continuum.

\textbf{Convergence.} Both designs detect a dead link the same way and through
the same node-aging timeout, so their recovery times are of the same order and
the steps that follow detection are largely matched (Sections~\ref{subsec:res-convergence}
and~\ref{subsec:l2-rerun} give the breakdown). The modest difference, about half
a second in favour of the Layer~2 method, is \emph{not} in the relay forwarding:
our relay hop N2\,$\to$\,N3 is a plain kernel \texttt{ip\_forward}, as immediate
as the Layer~2 MAC rewrite once the route is in place, and nothing has to be
``brought into service'' there. The difference is at the \emph{source}. Because
our data plane is connection-oriented, when the direct link fails N1 is left
holding a TCP connection to N3 that can no longer deliver, and its transmitter
can stall on that socket up to the one-second send timeout
(\texttt{SO\_SNDTIMEO}, Section~\ref{subsec:tcp-socket-config}) while the
buffered frames time out, before the stream settles onto the already-open
connection to N2. A connectionless
Layer~2 source has nothing to wind down: a frame addressed to a dead next hop
simply fails at the link layer, and the next frame is relayed as soon as the
route changes. This source-side connection state, not the relay, is the half
second the Layer~2 method saves. The cost is secondary while detection sets the
floor, but it would become the limiting factor if the aging timeout were
shortened, the point at which a lighter, connectionless transport at the source
would be the natural lever. The re-run on the same testbed bears this out
directly.

\textbf{Observability and stability.} The Layer~3 approach trades convergence
speed for two practical advantages. The relay path is visible to standard IP
tools, which simplifies debugging and operation, whereas the Layer~2 path is
invisible at the IP layer. And, in contrast to the bursty, unstable relayed
stream that our re-implementation of the Layer~2 method produced on this
single-collision-domain testbed (Section~\ref{subsec:l2-rerun}), the Layer~3
system completed every experiment without such disruption. We attribute this to
its TCP data plane and slot-gated source rather than to any failure of the
Layer~2 method itself, which the reference shows to function; the re-run
reported above documents this contrast first-hand.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:eval-summary}

This chapter set out to answer two questions: whether the Layer~3 system
delivers a usable teleoperation link, and whether the nodes keep to their TDMA
slots while doing so. Both are answered in the affirmative.

The system performs well. The control channel is responsive and loses no
packets, the video stream is delivered at its full rate, and when the direct
link fails the traffic is restored automatically through the relay in under
three seconds. Crucially, inserting the relay changes almost nothing: the
latency, the throughput, and the timing of the stream are practically the same
whether the video travels directly or through an intermediate node. This is the
central practical result, the relay is, in effect, invisible to the
application.

The slots are respected. The strongest evidence is that both the periodic
control beacons and the continuous video stream are seen to transmit only inside
their assigned slot, in the correct order, and that this holds when the traffic
is relayed. Taken together with the clean, loss-free latency, this is direct
confirmation that the nodes stay synchronised and do not interfere with one
another, which was the main goal of the work.

Compared with the earlier Layer~2 approach, the two achieve the same
slot-level behaviour. The Layer~2 design recovers from a failure slightly
faster, because it does not have to re-establish a connection, whereas the
Layer~3 design gains reliability, observability with standard tools, and a
stream that stayed stable throughout every experiment. The remaining
limitations, faster recovery and a fuller study of the relay under heavier
multi-hop load, are left as directions for future work.
