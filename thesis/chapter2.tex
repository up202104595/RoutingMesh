\chapter{Background and State of the Art}
\label{chap:background}

This chapter surveys the technical background underlying the routing mechanism
developed in this dissertation. It begins with the challenges of multi-hop
wireless communication and the MANET paradigm, then describes the TDMA
medium-access strategy and the RA-TDMAs+ protocol that governs the network
used in this work. The TDMA implementation framework built by
Almeida~\cite{almeida2023synchronization} is presented next, as it served as
the direct foundation for the implementation. The chapter concludes with a
review of MANET routing approaches and an analysis of the previous routing
work by Morais~\cite{morais2024synchronization}.

\section{Multi-hop Wireless Communication}
\label{sec:wireless}

\subsection{The Shared Medium Problem}

Wireless communication differs fundamentally from wired communication because
all nodes within radio range share the same physical medium. Two simultaneous
transmissions on the same frequency and channel corrupt each other at any
receiver within range of both senders, a condition known as a collision.
Managing access to the shared medium without collisions is the role of the
Medium Access Control (MAC) protocol.

IEEE 802.11 (WiFi), the standard used in this work, employs Carrier Sense
Multiple Access with Collision Avoidance (CSMA/CA). A node wishing to
transmit first listens to the channel; if the channel is detected as idle,
the node waits a randomly chosen backoff interval and then transmits. The
random backoff reduces the probability that two nodes attempt to transmit
simultaneously, but it does not eliminate it: when two nodes independently
select the same backoff value, their transmissions collide.

A subtler problem arises from the limited range of wireless radios. Two nodes
that are each within range of a common third node may be out of range of each
other, so neither can detect the other's transmissions. When both transmit
simultaneously, the frames collide at the common node without either sender
being aware. This is the hidden terminal problem, and it cannot be fully
resolved by CSMA/CA alone. Under high traffic load, repeated collisions and
backoffs cause significant throughput degradation and unpredictable latency,
making CSMA/CA unsuitable as the sole MAC mechanism for real-time
applications.

\subsection{Ad-hoc and Mobile Ad-hoc Networks}

A wireless ad-hoc network is formed autonomously by the communicating devices
themselves, without relying on access points, base stations, or any
pre-existing infrastructure. Each device is simultaneously a communication
endpoint and a potential relay for traffic destined to other nodes. When two
nodes are not within direct radio range, packets can still reach their
destination by traversing one or more intermediate relay nodes, a mechanism
known as multi-hop forwarding.

A Mobile Ad-hoc Network (MANET) extends this concept to scenarios where nodes
move freely~\cite{conti2014mobile}. Node mobility causes the set of available
wireless links to change continuously: a link that exists at one moment may
disappear moments later as a node moves out of radio range, and new links
appear when nodes move into range of each other. Routing protocols must
therefore adapt continuously, detecting link failures and computing alternative
paths without any centralised coordinator.

MANETs impose additional constraints beyond those of static ad-hoc networks.
Nodes are typically battery-powered, so energy efficiency in protocol design
matters. The topology can change faster than routing protocol convergence
times, creating transient periods during which some destinations are
unreachable. Security and privacy are also concerns given the open nature of
the medium, although these fall outside the scope of the present work.

Despite these challenges, MANETs are the enabling technology for a wide range
of operational scenarios: emergency coordination after infrastructure
destruction, military tactical communications in contested environments, and
teams of autonomous robots that must remain in contact while navigating freely
through complex environments. The last scenario is the direct motivation for
this work.

\subsection{Network Layering}

The networking functions relevant to this dissertation are organised according
to the layered model shared by both the OSI reference model and the TCP/IP
architecture. The \textbf{Physical layer} converts bits into signals
appropriate for the transmission medium (radio waves in the case of WiFi).
The \textbf{Data Link layer} controls access to the shared medium through the
MAC sub-layer and groups bits into frames; IEEE 802.11 CSMA/CA and RA-TDMAs+
operate at this layer. The \textbf{Network layer} handles logical addressing
and packet forwarding across multiple hops; IP operates here, and it is at
this layer that the routing mechanism developed in this dissertation installs
forwarding decisions into the kernel. The \textbf{Transport layer} provides
end-to-end reliability and flow control (TCP) or lightweight datagram delivery
(UDP). \textbf{Application layer} protocols make use of the transport
services; in this work the application is a real-time video stream.

The distinction between Layer 2 (data link) and Layer 3 (network) forwarding
is central to understanding the difference between the previous routing
approach by Morais and the one developed here, as discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:related-work}.

\section{Time Division Multiple Access and RA-TDMAs+}
\label{sec:ra-tdma}

RA-TDMAs+~\cite{oliveira2018clockless} is a TDMA protocol designed for
dynamic ad-hoc mesh networks. It achieves clockless relative
synchronisation without requiring GPS or any external time reference,
reduces interference among team members, and runs as an overlay on top of
the underlying IEEE 802.11 CSMA/CA hardware, which continues to handle
external interference from nodes outside the team. The protocol is the
product of combining the synchronisation method of RA-TDMAs~\cite{sousa2012ra}
with the topology-tracking and membership-management mechanisms of
RA-TDMA+~\cite{oliveira2018clockless}, taking the most effective features
of each.

\subsection{TDMA}

Time Division Multiple Access (TDMA) is a channel-sharing protocol that
partitions time into a repeating sequence of rounds, each subdivided into
$N$ equal slots. Every node in the network is assigned one unique slot per
round and is permitted to transmit data only within that slot. Because
transmissions are scheduled rather than contended, two nodes can never
occupy the channel simultaneously, eliminating collisions by construction.

If $T_{slot}$ is the slot duration and $N$ the number of active nodes,
the round period $T$ is:

\begin{equation}
T = N \times T_{slot}
\label{eq:frame}
\end{equation}

Figure~\ref{fig:tdma-round} illustrates the round structure for a
network with $N$ nodes, where the slots are numbered sequentially and
the sequence repeats periodically.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{fig_tdma_round}
\caption{TDMA round structure. Each node is assigned one numbered slot
per round; the round repeats with period $T$.}
\label{fig:tdma-round}
\end{figure}

A packet that arrives at the transmit queue must wait at most one full
round before it can be sent, giving a deterministic upper bound on
access latency. This property makes TDMA well suited to real-time
applications that require predictable timing, such as video streaming.

\subsection{RA-TDMA}

For a TDMA round to operate without slot overlaps, every participating
node must perceive slot boundaries in the same way. In infrastructure
networks, a base station broadcasts a reference clock that all devices
lock onto. In ad-hoc networks this centralised reference does not exist,
and a distributed synchronisation mechanism is needed instead.

RA-TDMA addresses this by having nodes synchronise through the timing
of each other's transmissions, without requiring any shared external
clock. Each node transmits a state packet at the beginning of its
designated slot. Receivers observe when those packets arrive and
compare the arrival time to the expected time, deriving a per-packet
delay estimate. These estimates are used to progressively adjust slot
boundaries so that overlaps are driven toward zero. The protocol can
be layered on top of any wireless communication technology.

Two versions of RA-TDMA are relevant to this work: RA-TDMAs~\cite{sousa2012ra},
which contributed the synchronisation algorithm, and
RA-TDMA+~\cite{oliveira2018clockless}, which contributed the
topology-tracking mechanism.

\subsection{RA-TDMAs: Synchronisation Algorithm}
\label{sec:ratdmas-sync}

RA-TDMAs~\cite{sousa2012ra} was originally developed to support video
streaming between Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) and a ground base
station over a multi-hop line topology. In this setting each UAV relays
the video stream one hop closer to the base station, up to a maximum of
five hops; each additional hop introduces forwarding delay that
accumulates at the destination.

The synchronisation mechanism works as follows. The header of each
transmitted packet carries the slot ID and the transmission offset
$p_k$, defined as the time elapsed since the start of the sender's
slot at the moment the packet was sent. A receiving node $j$ observes
the real arrival time $rx_k$ of packet $k$ from node $i$ and computes
the synchronisation delay $\delta_k$ as the difference between the
observed arrival and the arrival that would have occurred if the two
nodes were already perfectly synchronised:

\begin{equation}
\delta_k = (rx_k - \hat{rx}_k + T/2)_T - T/2
\label{eq:delay}
\end{equation}

The modular arithmetic, denoted by the subscript $T$, wraps the result
into the interval $(-T/2,\; T/2)$, disambiguating whether the packet
arrived early or late relative to the expected slot.

To compute $\hat{rx}_k$, the receiver must first estimate where node
$i$'s slot begins. Given that node $j$ knows its own slot start
$B^{(j)}$ and that slots are of equal length $s = T/n$ and ordered by
node ID, the expected slot start of node $i$ as seen by node $j$ is:

\begin{equation}
\hat{B}^{(i)}_k = (B^{(j)} - (j - i)\,s + T)_T,
\quad j, i \in [1, n]
\label{eq:expected-slot}
\end{equation}

Adding the transmission offset $p_k$ yields the expected packet arrival:

\begin{equation}
\hat{rx}_k = (\hat{B}^{(i)}_k + p_k)_T
\label{eq:expected-rx}
\end{equation}

Substituting into Equation~\ref{eq:delay} gives the full expression
for $\delta_k$:

\begin{equation}
\delta_k = (rx_k - (\hat{B}^{(i)}_k + p_k) + T/2)_T - T/2
\label{eq:delay-full}
\end{equation}

Before a node starts transmitting at the beginning of its slot, all
packet delay estimates recorded during the previous round are analysed
and a delay function $f$ is used to determine the shift to apply. Three
methods are defined for this analysis:

\begin{description}
\item[Minimum delay] Uses the smallest observed $\delta_k$. The slot
  advances by the least amount, making conservative corrections. This
  reduces the risk of advancing the slot so far that it overlaps with
  the preceding slot, but it is the slowest to converge.
\item[Maximum delay] Uses the largest observed $\delta_k$. The slot
  advances aggressively, reducing the chance of overlapping with the
  following slot, at the cost of a higher risk of encroaching on the
  preceding one.
\item[Median delay] Uses the median of all $\delta_k$ values,
  dampening the influence of outliers caused by occasional retransmissions
  or interference spikes. This balances the trade-offs of the two
  extreme approaches and is the strategy adopted in RA-TDMAs+.
\end{description}

The selected delay value is bounded to prevent excessively large
corrections, and slots can only be shifted forward (delayed), never
backwards:

\begin{equation}
\Delta(r) = \min\!\Bigl(\max\!\bigl(f(\delta_1,\,\delta_2,\,\ldots),\;0\bigr),\;
\Delta_{max}\Bigr)
\label{eq:shift}
\end{equation}

where $\Delta_{max}$ is a configurable ceiling, typically 25\% of the
slot duration. The slot start for the next round is updated as:

\begin{equation}
B^{(j)}(r) = B^{(j)}(r-1) + \Delta(r)
\label{eq:slot-start}
\end{equation}

All nodes continuously apply this adjustment until no delay is detected,
at which point the round structure has converged and transmissions no
longer overlap.

\subsection{RA-TDMA+: Topology and Membership}
\label{sec:ratdma-plus}

RA-TDMA+~\cite{oliveira2018clockless} extended the RA-TDMA approach to
fully dynamic mesh networks where the set of communicating nodes is not
fixed and not all pairs of nodes have a direct radio link. Two key
mechanisms were introduced: a round structure that adapts dynamically
to team membership changes, and a topology dissemination protocol that
gives every node a globally consistent view of the network.

The set of nodes currently active in the team is denoted $\tau_A$, and
the number of active nodes is $N = |\tau_A|$. Both quantities change
dynamically as nodes join or leave. The round period $T_{up}$ is
chosen according to the real-time requirements of the application and
remains fixed. Each node receives exactly one slot of duration
$t_{slot} = T_{up} / N$, so that as the team grows, slot duration
decreases proportionally, allowing new members to be absorbed without
changing the total round period. Slots are assigned in ascending order
of each node's unique numeric ID: the node with the smallest ID in
$\tau_A$ receives slot $s_0$, the next smallest $s_1$, and so on. For
example, if $\tau_A = \{\text{ID}_2, \text{ID}_3, \text{ID}_5\}$,
then $\text{ID}_2 \to s_0$, $\text{ID}_3 \to s_1$, and
$\text{ID}_5 \to s_2$~\cite{oliveira2018clockless}.

\subsubsection{State Dissemination: Flooding with Aggregation}

In a mesh network, not all nodes have a direct radio link to each
other. A node that is two or more hops away from another cannot
observe its transmissions directly. To overcome this, RA-TDMA+ uses a
flooding-with-aggregation mechanism.

At the start of each slot, the transmitting node broadcasts its current
state as a multicast message. This state includes its local view of
team membership, connectivity to neighbours, and an age variable for
each piece of information that tracks how recently it was updated.
Nodes that receive this broadcast merge the incoming state with their
own local state, always keeping the most recently timestamped entry for
each piece of data. At the start of their own slot, they rebroadcast
the merged state.

Through this process, information travels one hop per round. After a
number of rounds equal to the network diameter, every node has received
contributions from all others, achieving a globally consistent shared
state. The age variables prevent old data from overwriting more recent
updates as information circulates through the network.

Figure~\ref{fig:flooding} illustrates this process for a four-node
network in which node~4 joins via node~2. In round~1, node~4 broadcasts
its state to node~2, its only neighbour. In round~2, node~2 merges the
received state with its own and rebroadcasts, reaching nodes~1, 3, and~4.
After two rounds the entire network holds a consistent shared state.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{fig_flooding}
\caption{Flooding with aggregation. In round~1 (left), node~4 broadcasts
its state to its sole neighbour, node~2. In round~2 (right), node~2

\label{fig:flooding}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Topology Tracking}

The network topology is represented as a connectivity matrix $M$ of
size $N \times N$. Each entry $M_{jk}$ is a binary value indicating
whether node $j$ is currently receiving transmissions from node $k$:

\begin{equation}
M_{jk} = \begin{cases}
1 & \text{if node } j \text{ receives from node } k \\
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:matrix}
\end{equation}

Each node is solely responsible for maintaining its own row of $M$.
Before broadcasting, it updates row $j$ to reflect which neighbours
it has heard in the most recent rounds. When a matrix is received from
another node, only the rows for which the incoming age value is more
recent than the local copy are updated. This ensures that topology
changes propagate through the network within one round after they
occur, while stale information is never able to overwrite fresh data.

Figure~\ref{fig:connectivity} shows how the connectivity matrix evolves
when a new node joins the network. When node~2 joins and connects to
node~4, a new row and column are added to the matrix, and the updated
topology is reflected in the network graph.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{connectivity}
\caption{Update of the connectivity matrix and network topology after
node~2 joins the network. The matrix gains a new row and column,
and the link between node~2 and node~4 becomes visible to all nodes.}
\label{fig:connectivity}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Membership Tracking}

Keeping an accurate record of which nodes belong to $\tau_A$ is
essential for correct slot allocation. Node additions and removals are
detected through the connectivity matrix:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Node joins:} when a new node's row appears for the
    first time in a received matrix broadcast, the local copy is
    extended with a new row and column, $N$ is incremented, and the
    TDMA core is notified to reorganise the round. The updated layout
    propagates through the network in the following broadcast.

  \item \textbf{Node leaves:} when a node's row has not been refreshed
    for more than a threshold number of consecutive rounds (three rounds
    in the implementation used in this work), all nodes independently
    detect the departure, remove the corresponding row and column,
    decrement $N$, and contract the round accordingly.
\end{itemize}

Because every node applies the same deterministic rules to the same
shared state, all nodes converge to the same slot layout without
requiring any explicit coordination message.

\subsection{RA-TDMAs+: Combined Operation}

RA-TDMAs+ brings together the synchronisation algorithm of RA-TDMAs
(Section~\ref{sec:ratdmas-sync}) and the topology and membership
mechanisms of RA-TDMA+ (Section~\ref{sec:ratdma-plus}). The result
is a single protocol that provides collision-free medium access,
clockless distributed synchronisation, and a continuously updated
network topology view, all without any external reference or
centralised coordinator.

The two mechanisms interact through the spanning tree of the
connectivity matrix. The synchronisation algorithm requires each
node to observe delays from its neighbours, but in a mesh topology
with cycles, combining delays from multiple paths would create
synchronisation loops. RA-TDMAs+ resolves this by computing the
Minimum Spanning Tree of $M$ and filtering delay observations so
that only delays from links present in the spanning tree are used
in the computation of $\Delta(r)$.

\section{TDMA Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

The TDMA framework was originally developed by Lu\'{i}s Pinto and subsequently
refactored by Almeida~\cite{almeida2023synchronization} into a modular,
event-based architecture. The refactored implementation provides a user-space
implementation of RA-TDMAs+ on Linux, separating a core scheduling module from
a set of independently loaded plug-in modules, so that components can be added
or replaced without disrupting the core. The present work used this framework
as a starting point and introduced modifications to support the routing
mechanism described in subsequent chapters. The principal modules relevant to
this work are the topology-tracking module (\texttt{matrix.c}) and the
Synchroniser. Figure~\ref{fig:tdma-framework} shows the overall architecture.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{tdmaframeworkDiogo}
\caption{Architecture of the TDMA framework~\cite{almeida2023synchronization}.
The \texttt{TDMA\_Core} communicates with the application through the TUN
virtual device and with plug-in modules through AF\_UNIX local sockets
(red) and shared memory (green).}
\label{fig:tdma-framework}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Core Scheduling Module}

\texttt{TDMA\_Core} is the central component of the framework and is responsible for managing
medium access according to the TDMA slot schedule. It implements four concurrent threads.
The transmit thread (TX) is scheduled to wake at the beginning of the node's assigned slot and
remains dormant for the rest of the frame. When active, it dequeues pending packets, prepends
a TDMA header, and forwards them via a UDP socket over the wireless interface (\texttt{wlan0}).
The TDMA header carries the packet type, a global sequence number, the relative sending
timestamp based on the current round time, the node ID, and the current slot boundaries. As
noted in~\cite{almeida2023synchronization}, the header is 18 bytes in size and plays an essential
role in the RA-TDMAs+ protocol. The receive thread (RX) listens on the same UDP socket,
strips incoming TDMA headers, records the per-packet timing information required for
synchronisation, and returns payloads to the application layer. The event-handler thread
processes control events sent by plug-in modules over AF\_UNIX local sockets, such as updated
slot boundaries from the Synchroniser or membership changes from \texttt{matrix.c}. Finally,
the configuration thread manages the round parameters and coordinates the scheduling of the TX
thread.

Each module operates as a separate process and communicates with \texttt{TDMA\_Core} through
two IPC mechanisms: AF\_UNIX local sockets, used to send control events between modules and
the core, and shared memory, which allows essential data to be exchanged with minimal overhead.
\texttt{TDMA\_Core} holds exclusive write permissions over the shared memory it creates; if
another module needs to modify a shared variable, it must send a request event via AF\_UNIX.

The framework exposes the network to applications through a TUN virtual network interface
(\texttt{tun0}). TUN operates at Layer~3, handling IP packets as if they were passing through a
physical network interface card, unlike TAP which operates at Layer~2 and processes Ethernet
frames. IP packets sent by an application to \texttt{tun0} are read by \texttt{TDMA\_Core} in
user space, encapsulated with the TDMA header, and queued for transmission during the node's
slot. On the receive side, stripped payloads are injected back into \texttt{tun0} and delivered
to the destination application as ordinary IP datagrams, making the TDMA transport layer
transparent to higher layers. Figure~\ref{fig:tdma-header} illustrates the headers a packet
carries when leaving the TDMA framework.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{headerstdma}
\caption{Headers present in a packet leaving the TDMA framework. The application payload
(Transport and Application layers) is encapsulated with the TDMA header and transmitted via
a UDP socket over \texttt{wlan0}~\cite{almeida2023synchronization}.}
\label{fig:tdma-header}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Topology-Tracking Module}

The topology-tracking module (\texttt{matrix.c}) is essential for the correct operation of
RA-TDMAs+ and implements the connectivity matrix mechanism described in
Section~\ref{sec:ratdma-plus}. It runs as an independent plug-in process that communicates
with \texttt{TDMA\_Core} through shared memory and AF\_UNIX events.

To maintain a direct view of the network topology, each node must share the total number of
active nodes, their respective identifiers, the connectivity matrix containing link information
between nodes, and a per-row age vector that allows the most recent information to be retained.
These fields are stored in the \texttt{tdma\_matrix\_t} structure shown in
Listing~\ref{lst:matrix}, which is serialised into a compact beacon and broadcast via multicast
at the beginning of each node's slot. Figure~\ref{fig:matrix-packet} shows the layout of this
beacon packet.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{matrixpacket}
\caption{Structure of the matrix beacon packet broadcast by each node at the beginning of its
slot~\cite{almeida2023synchronization}.}
\label{fig:matrix-packet}
\end{figure}

\begin{lstlisting}[language=C,
    caption={Variables used to save the matrix information (\texttt{matrix.c})~\cite{almeida2023synchronization}},
    label={lst:matrix}]
typedef struct {
    uint8_t myId;
    uint8_t numberOfActiveNodes;
    uint8_t matrix[MNUM_DRONES][MNUM_DRONES];
    uint8_t idOfActiveNodes[MNUM_DRONES];
    double  creationTime[MNUM_DRONES];
    double  age[MNUM_DRONES];
} __attribute__((__packed__)) tdma_matrix_t;
\end{lstlisting}

Three functions govern the lifecycle of this module. \texttt{MATRIX\_init()} initialises all
necessary variables when the module loads. \texttt{MATRIX\_parsePkt()} parses received beacon
packets and updates the local matrix. \texttt{MATRIX\_share()} serialises and broadcasts the
local matrix to the network.

Before \texttt{MATRIX\_share()} transmits the local information, it first removes stale entries:
any row whose age exceeds three rounds is considered to belong to a node that has left the
network, and both its row and its slot assignment are removed. The remaining rows are then
aged by one, the local row is refreshed with the currently active links observed during the
preceding round, and the updated structure is serialised and broadcast.

When a packet is received, \texttt{MATRIX\_parsePkt()} deserialises it into a local
\texttt{tdma\_matrix\_t} variable and merges each incoming row into the local copy using the
age field to resolve conflicts: the row with the lower age value (more recently updated) is
retained. The total number of active nodes, their identifiers, and the slot order are updated as
necessary. If the slot assignment for the local node changes, the module notifies
\texttt{TDMA\_Core} via an AF\_UNIX event so that the schedule can be reorganised before
the next round. The matrix encodes the topology in binary form: a value of~1 indicates a direct
link between two nodes, while 0 indicates no connection.

After updating the matrix, the module runs Prim's algorithm on the connectivity graph to compute
a Minimum Spanning Tree (MST) and writes the result to a shared-memory region protected by
a mutex, as shown in Listing~\ref{lst:spanning}. This structure is consumed by the Synchroniser
to filter delay measurements and by higher-level components to derive forwarding decisions.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=C,
    caption={Variables stored in the shared memory for the Spanning Tree~\cite{almeida2023synchronization}},
    label={lst:spanning}]
typedef struct {
    pthread_mutex_t mut_spanningTree;
    uint8_t numberOfActiveNodes;
    uint8_t spanningTree[MNUM_DRONES][MNUM_DRONES];
    uint8_t idOfActiveNodes[MNUM_DRONES];
} spanningTreeSHM_t;
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Synchroniser Module}

The Synchroniser implements the RA-TDMAs+ slot-adjustment algorithm described in
Section~\ref{sec:ratdmas-sync}. It has access to the packet delays computed by
\texttt{TDMA\_Core} and to the Spanning Tree maintained by \texttt{matrix.c}, both exposed
through shared memory. The module remains dormant until three milliseconds before the end of
the current TDMA round. In this window it reads the accumulated delay values, determines the
shift $\Delta(r)$, and delivers it to \texttt{TDMA\_Core} before the round boundary, at which
point the slot limits are updated.

The Spanning Tree is used to filter the delay measurements and prevent topology loops from
corrupting the synchronisation algorithm. Only delays corresponding to links present in the
Spanning Tree between a transmitting node and the local synchronisation node are considered.
Once filtered, the delays are grouped by transmitter ID and averaged per group. The configured
delay selection method (minimum, maximum, or median across groups) is then applied to obtain
a single raw shift value, following the procedure described in Section~\ref{sec:ratdmas-sync}.

The $\Delta_{\max}$ parameter, set during the configuration phase, defines the maximum
allowable phase adjustment per round, preventing large single-round corrections from
destabilising the schedule. If the computed shift exceeds $\Delta_{\max}$, the value is clipped
to that limit, which accelerates convergence toward a global virtual round when the required
adjustment is large and provides fine-grained control when the required adjustment is small.
The clipped value is sent to \texttt{TDMA\_Core} as a slot-limits event via AF\_UNIX, and
\texttt{TDMA\_Core} adjusts the TX thread wake time accordingly at the round boundary.
Figure~\ref{fig:sync-module} illustrates the complete execution cycle of the Synchroniser.

\section{Routing in Mobile Ad-hoc Networks}
\label{sec:routing}

Routing is the process of determining paths along which data packets travel from a source node
to a destination across a network. In infrastructure networks this task is performed by dedicated
routers with stable connectivity; in a MANET every node must simultaneously act as a host and
a potential forwarder, and the topology may change continuously as nodes move. A routing
protocol for a MANET must therefore discover the current topology or a sufficient portion of it,
compute forwarding paths, install those paths so that packets can be forwarded hop by hop, and
update them whenever the topology changes, all without a central coordinator.

\subsection{Routing Challenges in MANETs}

The fundamental difficulty of MANET routing is adapting to an unpredictable and frequently
changing topology. Node mobility causes links to appear and disappear without warning: a link
that exists at one moment may vanish seconds later when a node moves out of radio range or is
obstructed. The routing mechanism must detect such failures rapidly and recompute affected
paths before the disruption is perceived by ongoing applications.

A second challenge is control overhead. Every routing update that must be propagated across
the network consumes bandwidth that is shared among all nodes in radio range. Control traffic
that floods the network competes directly with application data, so the ideal routing protocol
keeps control messages to the minimum necessary to maintain correct forwarding. The balance
between responsiveness and overhead depends heavily on the network size, the rate of topology
change, and the traffic patterns of the application.

A third challenge is the choice of routing metric. The simplest metric is hop count, which
minimises the number of relay nodes between source and destination. However, a path with
fewer hops is not necessarily better in wireless networks: a two-hop path using reliable links
may outperform a single direct but lossy link. More refined metrics incorporate link quality
information such as packet delivery ratios, received signal strength, or acknowledgement
statistics to prefer stable links over weak ones.

A fourth challenge specific to robotic and teleoperation applications is the latency budget for
route recovery after a link failure. A real-time video stream cannot tolerate prolonged
interruption: if routing convergence takes several seconds, the operator loses situational
awareness during that interval. The routing mechanism must therefore detect failures and
activate alternative paths within a time window that is small enough not to disrupt the mission,
typically on the order of one to two seconds.

\subsection{Proactive Routing Protocols}

Proactive routing protocols maintain a complete and up-to-date routing table at every node at
all times. Routes are always ready for immediate use and there is no latency penalty when a new
flow begins. The cost is that control traffic must be exchanged periodically, even during stable
periods when no application data is being sent.

\textbf{Destination-Sequenced Distance Vector (DSDV)} extends the classical Bellman-Ford
algorithm with a sequence number mechanism that prevents routing loops and stale entries.
Each routing table entry carries a sequence number assigned by the destination node; when a
node receives an update it retains the entry with the higher sequence number, ensuring that
more recent information always takes precedence. Updates are broadcast periodically as either
full table dumps or smaller incremental updates that list only changed entries. DSDV is simple
to implement and suppresses the count-to-infinity problem through its sequence numbering.
Its principal limitation is that periodic broadcasts consume bandwidth proportional to the
network size regardless of whether anything has changed, making it inefficient during stable
periods. Its main advantages are immediate route availability, low implementation complexity,
and rapid convergence after a link failure.

\textbf{Optimised Link State Routing (OLSR)} is a proactive link-state protocol that reduces
control traffic through Multipoint Relays (MPRs). Each node selects a minimal subset of its
one-hop neighbours that collectively cover all of its two-hop neighbourhood; these are its
MPRs. Control messages are still flooded throughout the network, but only MPR nodes
retransmit them, replacing the full flooding of classical link-state with a targeted relay that
substantially reduces the number of transmissions in dense networks. Nodes exchange Hello
messages to discover their neighbourhood and negotiate MPR selections; MPR nodes generate
Topology Control (TC) messages that allow every node to reconstruct a partial view of the
network graph. Dijkstra's algorithm is then applied to this graph to compute the routing table.

OLSR excels in large, dense networks where the MPR optimisation yields the greatest savings
in control overhead. In sparse networks with few two-hop neighbours the MPR mechanism
offers diminishing returns and the protocol's overhead approaches that of standard link-state
flooding. The protocol is also more complex to implement and to tune than DSDV, requiring
careful selection of Hello and TC intervals to balance responsiveness against overhead.

\subsection{Reactive Routing Protocols}

Reactive protocols, also called on-demand protocols, do not maintain routing tables for
destinations to which no traffic is flowing. A route is discovered only when a source node has
data to send and no valid route is cached. This eliminates continuous control overhead during
idle periods, but introduces a discovery delay at the start of each new flow that may be
significant in multi-hop networks.

\textbf{Dynamic Source Routing (DSR)} discovers routes by flooding a Route Request (RREQ)
message that accumulates in its header the list of every node it traverses. When the RREQ
reaches the destination or an intermediate node with a valid cached route, a Route Reply
(RREP) is returned along the reverse path, carrying the complete discovered route. Subsequent
data packets from the source embed the full sequence of intermediate hops directly in their
headers; each forwarding node reads its successor from the header without consulting a local
routing table. A Route Error (RERR) message is generated if an intermediate node detects that
a link on the active path has failed, notifying the source so that it can use an alternative
cached route or initiate a new discovery.

DSR requires no periodic transmissions and can exploit promiscuous listening to populate a
route cache from overheard traffic, reducing the number of explicit discoveries. The main
disadvantages are that full-path headers increase packet overhead as hop count grows, and that
in highly mobile networks the route cache becomes stale quickly, increasing the frequency of
rediscoveries and therefore the effective control overhead.

\textbf{Ad-hoc On-demand Distance Vector (AODV)} shares DSR's on-demand discovery model
using the same RREQ/RREP/RERR message types but stores only the next hop toward each
known destination at each intermediate node, keeping routing state in per-hop tables rather
than in packet headers. Sequence numbers borrowed from DSDV ensure that only fresh route
information is accepted, and routes that remain unused for longer than a configurable Active
Route Timeout are removed. When a link failure is detected, RERR messages propagate
upstream to notify affected sources, which may attempt local repair before initiating a full
rediscovery.

AODV avoids the per-packet header overhead of DSR and scales better to larger networks, but
still incurs the discovery latency inherent to all reactive approaches. In highly mobile
environments, the balance between route lifetime and rediscovery frequency requires careful
parameter tuning. Table~\ref{tab:protocols} provides a summary comparison of all four
protocols.

\subsection{Graph-based Routing and Minimum Spanning Trees}
\label{sec:mst-routing}

An alternative to the above protocol families is to derive routes directly from a global graph
representation of the network topology, without any routing-protocol-specific message exchange.
If such a graph is already available, the routing algorithm can recompute forwarding paths
locally at each topology update with no additional control traffic.

A Minimum Spanning Tree (MST) of a weighted undirected graph $G = (V, E, w)$ is a
subgraph $T \subseteq E$ that connects all vertices in $V$, contains no cycles, and minimises
the total edge weight:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MST}(G) = \underset{T \subseteq E,\; T \text{ spans } V}{\mathrm{argmin}}
\sum_{(u,v) \in T} w(u,v)
\label{eq:mst}
\end{equation}

When edge weights represent link costs (for example, the inverse of a link quality measure),
the MST selects the set of most reliable links that still connects all nodes. Because the MST is
a tree, there is exactly one path between any pair of nodes, which simplifies next-hop
derivation: the next hop from node $u$ toward destination $d$ is uniquely determined by the
tree structure without ambiguity or any further search.

Prim's algorithm~\cite{dijkstra1959note} constructs the MST incrementally, starting from an
arbitrary root node. At each step it selects the minimum-weight edge that connects the
partially built tree to an unvisited node and adds that node to the tree, repeating until all
$N$ nodes have been included. The time complexity is $O(N^2)$ for an adjacency-matrix
representation, which is negligible for the small networks (fewer than ten nodes) typical of
robotic deployments. The resulting tree is then traversed from each source node to produce a
next-hop table: for each destination, the first step on the unique tree path determines the
next-hop neighbour.

The MST approach has one notable limitation: it minimises the total weight summed across
all edges simultaneously, rather than optimising individual source-to-destination paths. For
some node pairs the tree path may therefore not be the globally shortest available path. In
practice, for small and well-connected robotic networks this difference is small, and the
simplicity of tree-based lookup is a practical advantage in resource-constrained embedded
implementations.

\subsection{Applicability to RA-TDMAs+}
\label{sec:routing-applicability}

The RA-TDMAs+ framework provides every node with a continuously updated, globally
consistent view of the full network topology through the MATRIX beacon exchanged at the
beginning of each slot. This property has direct implications for the selection of a routing
approach, as analysed by Morais~\cite{morais2024synchronization}.

\paragraph{Reactive protocols.}
Flooding a RREQ to discover routes is redundant when the topology is already known at every
node from the MATRIX beacon. Moreover, in a TDMA network each hop of a RREQ flood
must wait for the transmitting node's assigned slot before the request can be forwarded. A
three-hop discovery therefore requires at least three complete frame periods to complete. At a
typical frame period, this represents hundreds of milliseconds of unavoidable latency per
discovery, which is unacceptable for real-time video teleoperation. Additionally, the reactive
approach does not leverage the topology information that RA-TDMAs+ already disseminates,
wasting the infrastructure that the medium-access layer has already established.

\paragraph{Proactive protocols.}
The periodic sharing of the connectivity matrix in RA-TDMAs+ is itself a proactive topology
exchange, so proactive routing is the natural fit. However, standard proactive protocols
introduce redundancy. OLSR's Hello messages duplicate the neighbour-discovery function
already performed by the MATRIX beacon; the MPR selection mechanism reduces flooding
overhead but provides diminishing returns in the small networks typical of robotic deployments
where nearly all nodes are within two hops of each other. DSDV requires nodes to share a
routing table in addition to the MATRIX beacon, increasing the size of each slot transmission
without adding information that is not already present in the connectivity matrix.

\paragraph{Network size and computation time.}
An additional constraint comes from the TDMA slot structure. With a fixed round period $T$
and $N$ active nodes, each slot occupies $T/N$ seconds. As $N$ grows, both the slot duration
and the transmission window available for data shrink. The routing computation must therefore
complete within the processing window at the start of each slot, before the transmission phase
begins. Any algorithm that is too costly in time or processing would reduce the effective
transmission window, increasing packet loss. This imposes a preference for algorithms with
low time complexity.

\paragraph{Algorithm selection.}
Three graph algorithms are applicable to the connectivity matrix: Prim's algorithm for the
MST, Dijkstra's algorithm for shortest paths, and the Bellman-Ford algorithm used by DSDV.
Dijkstra's and Prim's both have $O(N^2)$ time complexity on an adjacency matrix, while
Bellman-Ford runs in $O(N^3)$, which becomes disadvantageous as the network grows.
Beyond complexity, the key observation is that \texttt{matrix.c} already computes a Minimum
Spanning Tree using Prim's algorithm and stores it in shared memory as part of the
synchronisation pipeline. A routing algorithm that directly consumes this existing Spanning
Tree therefore requires no additional graph computation, reusing infrastructure that is already
executed every round. Dijkstra's algorithm, by contrast, would require a separate graph
traversal and an additional shared-memory structure for the result, adding latency and
complexity without benefit in the small, well-connected networks of robotic deployments.

The MST-based approach, consuming the Spanning Tree already maintained by
\texttt{matrix.c}, is therefore the most natural fit for RA-TDMAs+: it requires no additional
control messages, adds no per-slot overhead, completes in negligible time, and converges within
one beacon round after any topology change. Morais~\cite{morais2024synchronization}
reached the same conclusion in her analysis and adopted Prim's MST as the basis for the first
routing implementation built on top of RA-TDMAs+.

\section{Previous Routing Work in RA-TDMAs+}
\label{sec:related-work}

Morais~\cite{morais2024synchronization} designed and implemented the first routing mechanism
for RA-TDMAs+ networks, establishing a complete Layer~2 routing architecture on top of the
TDMA framework. The work covered the full stack from algorithm selection and data structure
design to framework adaptation and practical validation on physical robotic hardware. This
section describes the architecture, its key design decisions, and the limitations that motivate
subsequent approaches.

\subsection{Layer 2 Routing Architecture}

The routing strategy adopted by Morais operates at the data link layer (Layer~2). Forwarding
decisions are implemented by manipulating the destination MAC address at each relay hop,
while the IP header, including both the source and destination IP addresses, remains unchanged
throughout the path. This makes the relay completely transparent to applications: the destination
receives the packet as if it had arrived directly from the source, regardless of the number of
intermediate hops.

For a node to forward a packet toward a destination it cannot reach directly, the Linux kernel
must map the destination IP address to the MAC address of the appropriate next-hop neighbour
in the ARP table. Custom ARP entries are injected using the \texttt{ioctl()} system call, which
redirects IP-level traffic to the correct next hop at the link layer without modifying the IP layer.
For relay nodes to forward transit packets rather than discard them, IP forwarding is enabled
via the \texttt{net.ipv4.ip\_forward = 1} kernel parameter. A new plug-in routing module was
developed to run within the TDMA framework alongside the existing modules. It accesses the
MAC address table and the Spanning Tree through shared memory, and updates the ARP table
via \texttt{ioctl()} system calls. Figure~\ref{fig:framework-ana} shows the complete architecture
after the addition of the routing module.

\subsection{Routing Matrix and Data Structures}

Routes are represented as a routing matrix derived from the Spanning Tree. Each node constructs
a local routing table that maps every network destination to a next-hop neighbour and its MAC
address. Because the Spanning Tree is cycle-free, there is exactly one path between any two
nodes, making the next-hop lookup unambiguous.

The routing matrix is implemented as a singly linked list. Each entry in the primary list
represents a node with a direct link to the local node; attached to each primary entry is a
secondary list of all nodes reachable through that direct neighbour. When the local node needs
to forward a packet to a destination, it traverses the primary list to find the entry whose secondary
list contains the destination identifier, and uses the MAC address stored with that primary entry
as the ARP next-hop. Figure~\ref{fig:linkedlist1} illustrates this structure for three example
source nodes.

\bigskip
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{linkedlist1}
\caption{Routing linked list for three example source nodes A, C, and G. Each primary entry
corresponds to a directly connected neighbour; its secondary list contains all nodes reachable
through that neighbour~\cite{morais2024synchronization}.}
\label{fig:linkedlist1}
\end{figure}
\bigskip

The linked list node structure is defined as follows:

\medskip
\begin{lstlisting}[language=C,

    label={lst:routinglist}]
typedef struct routingList_ {
    uint8_t id;
    uint8_t macAddress[MAC_BYTES];
    struct routingList_* next;

Each node stores the neighbour identifier, the corresponding MAC address, a pointer to the next
primary entry (\texttt{next}), and a pointer to the secondary list of reachable nodes
(\texttt{accessibleNodes}).

\subsection{Routing Table Update Algorithm}

When the Spanning Tree changes, the linked-list routing table must be updated efficiently.
Morais developed an incremental algorithm that avoids rebuilding the entire table from scratch
on every round. The procedure begins with a Depth-First Search (DFS) from the local node over
the updated Spanning Tree, enumerating all reachable nodes grouped by their first-hop neighbour.
The result for each primary neighbour is sorted by node identifier using \texttt{qsort}, producing
a sorted array that can be compared directly against the corresponding secondary linked list.

The comparison scans both structures in parallel. If an identifier appears in the sorted array
but not in the linked list, the node is new and must be added together with an \texttt{ioctl()}
ARP insertion. If an identifier appears in the linked list but not in the array, the node has
become unreachable and its entry is removed together with an \texttt{ioctl()} ARP deletion. If
an identifier appears in both, no action is needed. This linear comparison ensures that
\texttt{ioctl()} calls are issued only for entries that genuinely changed, keeping the total
processing time within the slot window regardless of network size. Changes to the primary list
(direct neighbours) trigger removal of all secondary entries associated with the old neighbour
before the list is rebuilt from the DFS output.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Packet Routing via TUN and iptables}

In the TDMA framework, the TUN interface (\texttt{tun0}) operates on a separate IP subnet
(192.168.3.X) from the wireless ad-hoc interface \texttt{wlan0} (192.168.2.X), where X is the
node identifier. Applications must send packets addressed to the \texttt{wlan0} subnet so that
routing and ARP manipulation can take effect. To redirect application-generated packets from
\texttt{wlan0} to \texttt{tun0} transparently, iptables mangle rules are used.

The OUTPUT chain marks all outgoing \texttt{wlan0} packets with a non-zero mark, then
resets the mark to zero for TDMA framework packets identified by their fixed UDP port number
(4000 + node~ID). A policy routing rule then sends all marked packets to a secondary routing
table that routes them through \texttt{tun0}. This mechanism ensures that only application
traffic enters the TDMA framework, while the framework's own UDP transmissions are excluded
to prevent routing loops.

When a packet arrives at \texttt{tun0} at the destination node, it must be delivered back to the
application on \texttt{wlan0}. A raw socket of type \texttt{IPPROTO\_RAW} reads the packet
from \texttt{tun0} and re-injects it toward the destination IP address, causing the kernel to
route it to the \texttt{wlan0} interface via the standard routing table. Binding the raw socket to
the destination address rather than directly to the interface allows the kernel to handle
fragmentation and reassembly normally.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Experimental Validation and Limitations}

Morais validated the system on physical AlphaBot2-Pi robotic platforms, successfully
demonstrating video streaming over a three-node relay topology and confirming that MST-based
proactive routing is viable within the RA-TDMAs+ framework.

Despite this success, the Layer~2 approach has limitations that motivate alternative
architectures. Standard IP diagnostic tools such as \texttt{traceroute} and \texttt{ping} operate
at the IP layer and cannot observe MAC-level forwarding, making the relay path invisible to
them and complicating debugging. Linux networking subsystems operating at the IP layer,
including traffic shaping with \texttt{tc}, policy routing tables, and firewall rules with
\texttt{iptables}, cannot interact with a forwarding mechanism that operates below them.
The implementation also exhibited system-level instability during operation, with runtime
failures of undetermined origin. Finally, the source code was not made available for inspection
or reuse; only the TDMA framework of Almeida~\cite{almeida2023synchronization} was
accessible as a starting point for subsequent work.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:background-summary}

This chapter established the theoretical and practical background for the routing work described
in the remainder of this dissertation. The need for multi-hop routing arises from the limited
radio range of individual nodes in a MANET, combined with the continuous topology changes
caused by node mobility. RA-TDMAs+ addresses the medium access and synchronisation
problems through a clockless distributed TDMA protocol that also disseminates a continuously
updated connectivity matrix to all nodes via the MATRIX beacon. This matrix provides a global
view of the topology that serves as the direct foundation for routing without requiring any
additional control traffic.

Among the routing approaches reviewed, reactive protocols were ruled out because route
discovery is redundant given the already-available topology and because TDMA slot scheduling
introduces prohibitive per-hop discovery latency. Standard proactive protocols add control
overhead that duplicates functionality already present in the MATRIX beacon. A proactive
MST-based strategy that operates directly on the existing Spanning Tree maintained by
\texttt{matrix.c} was identified as the natural choice, reusing available infrastructure with
negligible additional computation. This is the same conclusion reached by
Morais~\cite{morais2024synchronization}, whose Layer 2 routing implementation on
AlphaBot2-Pi robots provided the first experimental validation of MST-based routing in
RA-TDMAs+ networks. Her work demonstrated multi-hop video streaming in practice and
defined the routing matrix, linked-list data structures, and ARP-based forwarding mechanism
that constitute the reference architecture for this research area. The limitations of the Layer 2
approach, in particular the invisibility of relay paths to standard IP tools and the absence of
integration with Linux networking subsystems, motivate the exploration of an alternative
architecture, described in subsequent chapters.
