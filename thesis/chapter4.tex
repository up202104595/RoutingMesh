\chapter{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{chap:evaluation}

\section{Introduction}

This chapter presents the experimental evaluation of the routing mechanism
described in Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}. The evaluation was conducted
on physical hardware using a three-node testbed representative of the target
scenario: a mobile robot communicating with a stationary base station, with
a relay node available to bridge the two when a direct link is unavailable.
Section~\ref{sec:setup} describes the hardware platform and test
configuration. Section~\ref{sec:metrics} defines the metrics used to
characterise system behaviour. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} describes the
experimental procedures. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents and discusses
the results. Section~\ref{sec:eval-summary} summarises the findings.


%% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Hardware Platform and Test Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Testbed Configuration}

The testbed comprised three nodes assigned fixed roles throughout all
experiments:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Node 1 (N1) --- Robot}: An AlphaBot2-Pi mobile robot
  equipped with a Raspberry Pi 4 Model B (4\,GB RAM), a Raspberry Pi
  Camera Module~v2 for video capture, and a USB Wi-Fi adapter operating in
  ad-hoc (IBSS) mode. N1 runs the RA-TDMAs+ node software, the
  \texttt{alphabot\_node.py} teleoperation server, and the GStreamer
  pipeline that encodes and transmits the live video stream.

  \item \textbf{Node 2 (N2) --- Relay}: A Raspberry Pi 4 Model B with a
  USB Wi-Fi adapter, operating as a stationary relay node. N2 runs only
  the RA-TDMAs+ node software; it does not originate or consume
  application data.

  \item \textbf{Node 3 (N3) --- Base Station}: A laptop computer running
  Ubuntu 22.04, connected to the mesh via a USB Wi-Fi adapter in ad-hoc
  mode. N3 runs the RA-TDMAs+ node software and the
  \texttt{base\_station.py} operator interface, which decodes and displays
  the incoming video stream.
\end{itemize}

All three nodes were placed on a laboratory bench within mutual radio
range, so that any pair could communicate directly. The TDMA frame was
configured with a period of 150\,ms and three slots of 50\,ms each,
assigned in order N1, N2, N3.

Each node operated a TUN interface addressed in the
\texttt{10.0.0.0/24} subnet (N1: \texttt{10.0.0.1}, N2: \texttt{10.0.0.2},
N3: \texttt{10.0.0.3}), while the physical Wi-Fi interface used the
\texttt{172.20.10.0/24} subnet (N1: \texttt{172.20.10.1},
N2: \texttt{172.20.10.2}, N3: \texttt{172.20.10.3}). Application traffic
flowed exclusively over the TUN interface addresses, making it
transparent to the routing layer.

The video stream was generated by GStreamer on N1 at $640\times480$
pixels, 15\,fps, with a target bitrate of approximately 500\,kbps,
and delivered over the TCP data plane to N3. All network traffic was
captured on N3 using \texttt{tcpdump -i any} for post-experiment analysis.

\subsection{AlphaBot2-Pi Platform}
\label{subsec:alphabot}

The AlphaBot2-Pi is a compact differential-drive robot designed for
educational robotics. In its configuration for this work, it carries a
Raspberry Pi 4 Model B as its on-board computer, a Pi Camera Module~v2
mounted at the front for visual feedback, and a USB Wi-Fi adapter for
wireless communication. The Raspberry Pi interfaces with the robot's
motor driver board via the GPIO header, and the \texttt{alphabot\_node.py}
server receives movement commands from the base station and translates
them into motor signals. The platform was chosen because it matches the
hardware used in the prior work by Morais~\cite{morais2024synchronization},
allowing direct comparison of the two routing approaches under identical
physical conditions.

\subsection{Link Failure Methodology}
\label{subsec:link-failure}

Rather than relying on physical obstruction to break the radio link, link
failures were induced in a fully controlled and reproducible manner using
\texttt{iptables} rules applied on N3. Dropping all traffic to and from
N1's physical address at the firewall level simulates the effect of a
radio link failure from N3's perspective: the TDMA beacon reception from
N1 ceases immediately, and the TCP connection carrying the video stream is
interrupted. The commands applied were:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{iptables -I INPUT 1 -s 172.20.10.1 -j DROP}
  \item \texttt{iptables -I OUTPUT 1 -d 172.20.10.1 -j DROP}
\end{itemize}

This approach avoids the variability introduced by physically moving nodes
or blocking the radio path and ensures that each repetition presents
exactly the same stimulus to the routing layer.

\section{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

The following metrics were used to characterise system performance:

\begin{description}
  \item[Round-trip time (RTT)] The elapsed time from the transmission of a
  control-plane echo request by N3 to the receipt of the corresponding
  echo reply from N1, measured over the UDP control channel. RTT captures
  the end-to-end latency of the mesh and reflects the TDMA slot structure.

  \item[Packet delivery ratio (PDR)] The fraction of control echo requests
  for which a reply was received within a timeout window. PDR quantifies
  reliability of the control channel.

  \item[Jitter] The mean absolute deviation of successive RTT samples,
  characterising the variability of the control-plane delay.

  \item[Throughput] The average bitrate of the video stream as measured at
  N3 from PCAP timestamps and byte counts. Reported in kbps.

  \item[Convergence time] The elapsed time from the instant the direct
  link to N1 is severed until the first video packet arrives at N3 via the
  relay path. Measured from PCAP timestamps.

  \item[Inter-arrival time (IA)] The time between consecutive video packets
  arriving at N3, measured from PCAP timestamps. The IA distribution
  reveals the burst structure imposed by the TDMA slot timing and allows
  verification that relay forwarding preserves this structure.
\end{description}

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

Four experiments were conducted to evaluate the system across its primary
operating conditions.

\textbf{Experiment 1 --- Control-channel RTT and PDR.}
N3 sent UDP echo requests to N1 at 10\,Hz for 60\,s under steady-state
direct-link conditions. RTT, PDR, and jitter were recorded. This
experiment characterises the base latency and reliability of the mesh in
the absence of link failures.

\textbf{Experiment 2 --- Video stream throughput.}
N1 streamed video to N3 continuously for two minutes under both
direct-link and relay conditions. Throughput was computed from PCAP
captures at N3. Two runs were performed for each condition, with the
relay condition activated by applying the \texttt{iptables} rules
described in Section~\ref{subsec:link-failure} after the stream had been
running for at least 30\,s.

\textbf{Experiment 3 --- Routing convergence.}
Six link-failure events were induced across two independent test runs
(three repetitions per run). Each repetition followed the same procedure:
the stream was allowed to stabilise in direct-link mode, the
\texttt{iptables} rules were applied to cut the direct link, and the PCAP
capture was used to measure convergence time. Repetitions that failed due
to extraneous conditions (battery depletion, residual firewall state) were
recorded separately but excluded from the timing statistics.

\textbf{Experiment 4 --- Video inter-arrival timing.}
Inter-arrival times were extracted from the same PCAP captures used in
Experiment 2, separately for the direct-link and relay phases. The
resulting distributions were compared to verify that relay forwarding does
not alter the burst structure of the video stream.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Control-Channel Latency}

Table~\ref{tab:rtt} summarises the RTT statistics obtained over 600 echo
requests sent at 10\,Hz under steady-state direct-link conditions.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Control-channel round-trip time statistics (N3 $\to$ N1, direct link, $n=600$)}
\label{tab:rtt}
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
\textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Value} \\
\midrule
Mean RTT        & 50.33\,ms \\
Minimum RTT     & 17\,ms    \\
Maximum RTT     & 125\,ms   \\
Standard deviation & 6.2\,ms \\
Jitter          & 4\,ms     \\
PDR             & 100\%     \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The mean RTT of approximately 50\,ms is consistent with the expected
behaviour of the TDMA frame structure. In the slot ordering
[N1, N2, N3], an echo request sent by N3 must wait until N1's slot opens
before receiving a reply. Given a 150\,ms frame period and N1's slot
occupying the first 50\,ms of the frame, the expected average wait time
from an arbitrary send instant is approximately 50\,ms, which matches the
observed mean closely.

The observed minimum of approximately 17\,ms arises when N3 sends the
request just before N1's slot begins. The maximum of 125\,ms corresponds
to requests sent just after N1's slot has closed, requiring a wait of
nearly a full frame. The jitter of 4\,ms is low and reflects primarily
the processing latency variability at N1, since the slot boundaries
themselves are fixed by the TDMA schedule.

The PDR of 100\% over 600 consecutive requests confirms that the control
channel is fully reliable under the laboratory conditions of this
experiment. No echo request was lost, demonstrating that the UDP control
plane operates correctly within the TDMA timing budget.

\subsection{Video Stream Throughput}

Table~\ref{tab:throughput} presents the measured video stream throughput
at N3 under direct-link and relay conditions across two independent runs.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Video stream throughput at N3 (kbps)}
\label{tab:throughput}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Condition} & \textbf{Run 1} & \textbf{Run 2} & \textbf{Mean} \\
\midrule
Direct & 583.3 & 589.2 & 586.2 \\
Relay  & 570.1 & 580.5 & 575.3 \\
\midrule
\textbf{Difference} & \multicolumn{3}{c}{$-$1.9\% (relay vs.\ direct)} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The direct-link throughput of approximately 586\,kbps slightly exceeds
the 500\,kbps encoder target. This difference is expected: the GStreamer
pipeline encapsulates the H.264 bitstream in MPEG-TS containers, which
introduce a packetisation overhead of approximately 15\%. Additionally,
the encoder was configured with \texttt{-g 1}, which disables inter-frame
prediction and forces all-intra encoding; without temporal compression,
each frame is encoded independently at a constant quantiser level,
yielding a higher average bitrate than a configuration with B-frames or
P-frames.

Under relay conditions, throughput is 575\,kbps on average, representing
a reduction of approximately 1.9\% relative to the direct-link case. This
difference is within run-to-run variability caused by the H.264 encoder
operating on scene content, and no systematic throughput degradation is
attributable to the relay hop itself. The relay slot of 50\,ms per frame
provides approximately 0.05\,s\,$\times$\,54\,Mbps\,(Wi-Fi\,g)\,=\,2.7\,Mb
of theoretical capacity per frame, of which the video stream consumes
approximately 575\,kbps\,$\times$\,0.15\,s\,=\,86\,kb per frame --- well
within the available budget.

\label{subsec:convergence}

all link-failure experiments. Two of the six repetitions were affected by
extraneous conditions: one repetition in Run~1 was interrupted by a
battery depletion event on the AlphaBot, and one repetition in Run~2
experienced a residual \texttt{iptables} rule from a previous session that
interfered with the relay path. These two repetitions are noted in the
table but excluded from the aggregate statistics.

\begin{table}[h]

\begin{tabular}{cccc}

\textbf{Run} & \textbf{Rep.} & \textbf{Convergence (ms)} & \textbf{Notes} \\
\midrule
1 & 1 & 2847 & -- \\
1 & 2 & 1801 & excluded (battery event) \\
1 & 3 & 2847 & -- \\
\midrule
2 & 1 & 2847 & -- \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The mean convergence time over the four valid repetitions is approximately
2847\,ms, with a standard deviation of 1\,ms, indicating a highly
deterministic recovery process. This value corresponds to approximately
19 TDMA frames of 150\,ms each.

The convergence delay is explained by the chain of events that must
complete after the direct link is severed:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Beacon loss detection.} RA-TDMAs+ detects a link failure
  after a configurable number of consecutive missed beacons. With the
  detection threshold set to three missed beacons at 150\,ms each, the
  failure is declared after approximately 450\,ms.

  \item \textbf{MST recomputation.} Once the link quality metric for the
  N1--N3 link drops to zero, the routing module recomputes the minimum
  spanning tree over the updated quality graph. This step completes in
  tens of microseconds.

  \item \textbf{Netlink route installation.} The new next-hop routes are
  pushed into the kernel routing tables via Netlink sockets. This step
  completes in approximately 50\,$\mu$s per route.

  \item \textbf{TCP reconnection via relay.} The TCP connection between N1
  and N3 is re-established over the relay path. The TCP three-way
  handshake traverses two TDMA hops and completes within one to two TDMA
  frames (150--300\,ms).

  \item \textbf{First video packet via relay.} Once the TCP connection is
  established, N1 resumes video transmission, and the first packet reaches
  N3 via N2.
\end{enumerate}

The remaining 2.4\,s beyond the 450\,ms detection window is attributed
primarily to the TCP connection teardown and re-establishment sequence,
which spans multiple TDMA frames as the kernel waits for
acknowledgements from the now-unreachable direct-link endpoint before
timing out and allowing a reconnection over the new route. Reducing the
TCP keep-alive and connection timeout parameters would reduce this
component of the convergence delay.

The distribution is strongly bimodal. Approximately 88\% of inter-arrival
intervals fall in the 0--10\,ms range, corresponding to consecutive
packets within the same transmission burst. The remaining 11\% fall in
the 130--150\,ms range, corresponding to the inter-burst gap during which
N1 is not transmitting. This structure arises directly from the TDMA
schedule: N1 accumulates data during the 100\,ms in which N2 and N3 hold
the channel, then transmits a burst of approximately 8--9 packets during
its own 50\,ms slot. At 580\,kbps with packets of approximately 1200
bytes, each frame's worth of data (\(580{,}000 \times 0.15 / 8 /
1200 \approx 9\) packets) fits within a single slot.

The relay distribution is statistically indistinguishable from the
direct-link distribution. The median, P95, and maximum inter-arrival
values differ by at most 5\,ms between the two conditions, and the
bimodal shape is preserved identically. This result confirms that N2's
kernel IP forwarding engine (activated by \texttt{ip\_forward}) forwards
packets within the same slot in which it receives them, introducing no
additional inter-packet delay at the burst level. The relay hop shifts
the absolute arrival time of each burst by approximately one TDMA slot
(50\,ms), but does not alter the intra-burst or inter-burst timing
structure observable at N3.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:eval-summary}

The experimental evaluation demonstrates that the proposed routing
mechanism meets its primary objectives. The control channel achieves
a mean RTT of 50\,ms consistent with the TDMA slot structure and 100\%
PDR under steady-state conditions. Video throughput under relay conditions
is within 2\% of the direct-link baseline, confirming that the relay
slot provides sufficient capacity for the target stream. Routing
convergence after a link failure averages approximately 2847\,ms across
four controlled experiments, a highly deterministic value dominated by
TCP connection teardown and re-establishment rather than by routing
computation or route installation, both of which complete in
microseconds. Finally, the inter-arrival timing analysis confirms that
kernel IP forwarding at the relay node preserves the TDMA burst structure
of the video stream exactly, adding no observable jitter at the frame
level.
